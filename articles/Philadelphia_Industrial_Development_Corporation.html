The Philadelphia Industrial Development Corporation (PIDC) is a nonprofit that works with the City of Philadelphia to attract and retain businesses, finance development projects and manage industrial real estate. Founded in 1958 by the City and the Greater Philadelphia Chamber of Commerce, PIDC describes its aim as to spur investment, support business growth and foster development that creates jobs, revitalizes neighborhoods and drives growth to every corner of Philadelphia.[1] It is neither a city department nor a state authority, though it works closely with the Commerce Department and with city-chartered bodies.

1 Structure and leadership

PIDC is governed by a board of civic and business leaders and is run by a president. Jodie Harris was named the 12th president in February 2023 and began on June 1, 2023; she was the first African American woman to lead PIDC in its then 65-year history. She had previously been director of the U.S. Treasury's Community Development Financial Institutions Fund.[2] The organization publishes annual reviews, with a 2025 annual review released in 2026, that describe its lending, land sales and capital projects.

2 PAID and public assets

PIDC staffs and manages assets owned by the Philadelphia Authority for Industrial Development (PAID), a city-created public authority that holds title to certain industrial and commercial properties. PIDC's organizational materials list PAID's board alongside its own structure.[3] The best-known asset is the Philadelphia Navy Yard in South Philadelphia, a former naval shipyard redeveloped into a business and institutional campus; PIDC has also managed city-owned industrial land elsewhere, which it sells or leases for manufacturing, logistics and other uses. Because these are public assets, sales and leases follow public-authority rules, and the use of publicly owned land is also shaped by City Council and local politics (see Councilmanic Prerogative).

3 Programs and financing

PIDC provides several kinds of support. It makes loans to small and mid-sized businesses, sometimes with tax-exempt bond financing or federal and state programs; assembles and prepares industrial sites; and provides technical assistance on site selection. It also supports business-friendly programs such as those linked to the America 250 commemoration of 2026, with guidance for local businesses.[3] Financing frequently interacts with tax incentives (see Tax Abatement and Development Incentives) and with labor standards that apply to publicly financed construction. For small business supports and licensing, see also Office of Economic Opportunity.

4 Relationship to city government

The mayor's administration sets economic development priorities, and the Commerce Department leads the city's business attraction and retention efforts, often with PIDC acting as the transaction arm that closes deals, holds land and arranges financing. The Redevelopment Authority handles condemnation and redevelopment areas, while the Philadelphia Housing Development Corporation focuses on housing. Other partners include business improvement districts, the Chamber of Commerce for Greater Philadelphia and anchor institutions.

5 Accountability and debates

Because PIDC is a nonprofit, it is not directly subject to every transparency law that applies to city agencies, but PAID and other public bodies it serves are subject to the Sunshine Act and Right-to-Know Law, and its use of public funds can be examined by the City Controller and funders. Debates about PIDC are the same as debates about economic development generally: whether public land and subsidies should prioritize large employers, whether jobs go to local residents, how neighborhoods benefit, and how industrial land should be balanced against housing demand (see Developers and the Real Estate Lobby and Community Development Corporations).
