Philanthropy in Philadelphia
TypePrivate and community foundations, donor-advised funds, corporate giving
Largest local funderWilliam Penn Foundation (reported assets about $3.4 billion; about $148.8 million in giving in 2024)
Community foundationThe Philadelphia Foundation (reported assets about $900 million)
Other major namesThe Pew Charitable Trusts; Lenfest Institute for Journalism; Knight Foundation; corporate foundations

Philanthropy in Philadelphia is the network of private foundations and donors whose grants support schools, arts institutions, parks, housing, health and civic organizations. Because the city government and the School District operate under tight budgets, foundation money often shapes which pilot programs, studies and nonprofits get started. Foundations are private actors, not government bodies; their influence comes from funding decisions rather than legal authority. [1]

1 Major funders

The William Penn Foundation, established in 1945, is described by the Inquirer as the biggest foundation focused solely on Greater Philadelphia and, after other funders such as the Annenberg Foundation moved their focus elsewhere, has assumed primacy among local funders. Its annual giving grew from about $110 million in 2020 to about $149 million in 2024, according to a grant-database summary of its filings. The Pew Charitable Trusts, based in Philadelphia, has billions in assets but directs most grants nationally and internationally. The Philadelphia Foundation, the regional community foundation, manages donor-advised and designated funds and awards tens of millions of dollars annually. The Lenfest Foundation, created by the late Gerry Lenfest, gave away more than $1.3 billion but was designed as a spend-down vehicle rather than a perpetual one. [2]

2 Funding priorities

In 2024 the William Penn Foundation announced changes to what it funds, and its May 2026 docket reported 128 grants totaling about $57.2 million, with 41 percent in children and families. Local philanthropy typically concentrates in education, early childhood, arts and culture, parks and public spaces (for example through the Fairmount Park Conservancy), neighborhood development and, increasingly, housing and racial-equity work. [3]

3 Funding the public sector and media

Foundation grants have supported Mural Arts Philadelphia, the Free Library and Parks and Recreation through friends groups and conservancies. Philanthropy also underwrites local journalism: the Lenfest Institute owns the Philadelphia Inquirer and has invested more than $117 million in news initiatives, as it reports (see Local News Media in Philadelphia). Foundation-supported groups such as community development corporations, legal-aid organizations and good-government groups rely on grants and, in some cases, on City contracts.

4 Debates about foundation power

Observers of philanthropy raise recurring questions. Supporters say foundations can take risks government cannot, fund long-term projects and convene stakeholders. Critics argue that a small number of funders set priorities with limited public input, that grantee organizations may avoid criticizing their funders, and that grant-driven programs may not last. Foundations typically respond with community advisory structures and published grant lists. Grassroots groups have sometimes called for more unrestricted, community-led funding. These debates arise in education reform, charter-school funding and neighborhood redevelopment (see Charter Schools in Philadelphia).

5 Transparency and access

Private foundations file annual Form 990-PF returns with the IRS, which are public through services such as ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer. Most local foundations publish grant lists and application guidelines on their websites, and many fund only invited or pre-qualified applicants. Residents seeking funding for neighborhood projects can start with the Philadelphia Foundation and neighborhood-focused funds.
