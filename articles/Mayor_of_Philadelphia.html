The Mayor of Philadelphia is the chief executive officer of a city of about 1.6 million people and of the county government that the city absorbed in 1854. Cherelle Parker, a former state representative and Council member, took office on January 1, 2024 as the 100th mayor and the first woman elected to the post. The mayor presides over a workforce of roughly 29,000 employees, as reported, and proposes the annual budget, but shares power with an independent City Council and several elected officials.[1]

1 Election and term limits

The mayor is elected in odd-numbered years before the presidential cycle, in November, after a party primary in May. Because Philadelphia has long had a Democratic voter-registration majority, the Democratic primary is usually decisive (see Elections in Philadelphia). The Charter limits mayors to two consecutive four-year terms; a former mayor can run again after sitting out a term. The mayor is the only city official subject to a term limit. A "resign-to-run" rule requires Council members and other city officials to resign before running for mayor.[2] See Mayors of Philadelphia Timeline.

2 Powers

The Charter vests executive power in the mayor. Key powers include:

  • Appointment: the mayor appoints the Managing Director, the Finance Director, the City Solicitor and most department heads, plus members of many boards (see Mayor's Cabinet and Offices). Civil service positions are filled through the merit system (see Civil Service Commission).
  • Budget: the mayor submits the operating and capital budgets and a five-year plan to Council in March (see Philadelphia City Budget Process). For FY2027, Parker proposed a $6.97 billion budget in March 2026, and Council approved the FY2027 operating and capital budgets on June 11, 2026.[3]
  • Veto and signature: the mayor signs or vetoes ordinances; Council can override a veto with a two-thirds vote (12 of 17).
  • Executive orders and collective bargaining: the administration negotiates with city unions (see Public Sector Bargaining in Pennsylvania) and issues executive orders directing departments.
  • Emergency authority: the mayor can declare a disaster emergency, coordinated through the Office of Emergency Management.

3 Relationship with Council

The mayor cannot spend money Council has not appropriated and cannot raise taxes alone. Council sets tax rates by ordinance, often within authority granted by the state. Tensions arise over land use, where district Council members hold informal control (see Councilmanic Prerogative), and over taxes: in spring 2026 Council members clashed publicly with the mayor and the School District over school closures and a proposed $1-per-ride tax on ride-hailing services, according to the Inquirer and Billy Penn.

4 Limits on the office

The mayor does not control the independently elected row offices, the courts, or state-created authorities such as SEPTA. Pennsylvania law limits what the city may tax or regulate (see Home Rule Limits and State Preemption), and the PICA oversight board reviews five-year plans. The mayor appoints members of the Board of Education but does not directly run the school district.

5 The Parker administration

Parker's agenda has emphasized public safety, housing, and neighborhood cleanliness under the label "One Philly." Her FY2027 budget was titled "One Philly, One Future." Managing Director Adam Thiel's departure and the naming of Carlton Williams as his successor were announced on July 28, 2026, according to local reports. Her administration has also dealt with federal funding and immigration pressures (see Federal Government and Philadelphia).
