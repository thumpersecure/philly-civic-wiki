Police accountability systems in Philadelphia are a set of overlapping processes rather than a single office. A single incident may be examined by the police department's own investigators, by the civilian oversight commission, by the District Attorney as a possible crime, by a civil court, and, in rare cases, by federal authorities. Which path applies depends on the allegation, and each path has different standards of proof, different decision makers and different outcomes.

1 Internal Affairs and the Police Board of Inquiry

The Philadelphia Police Department investigates complaints through its Internal Affairs Division (IAD), staffed by sworn investigators. IAD reviews allegations of misconduct, use of force and policy violations, and sends findings up the chain of command. For serious cases the Police Commissioner may refer an officer to the Police Board of Inquiry (PBI), an internal hearing panel that hears evidence from the department and the officer and makes findings and a disciplinary recommendation; the Commissioner then decides the penalty, up to dismissal. The Citizens Police Oversight Commission (CPOC) published a report on the PBI's functioning in collaboration with the department in 2021 and 2023 materials, identifying issues in the disciplinary process. [1] Officers may challenge discipline through the union grievance procedure.

2 Civilian oversight

Voters created the Citizens Police Oversight Commission in 2020 to replace the Police Advisory Commission. CPOC receives complaints, reviews the department's investigations, audits policy compliance, and analyzes data. Its authority to run independent investigations has been contested: interest arbitrators in 2025 did not grant that power in the police contract, according to Axios reporting, and CPOC says it is currently barred from such investigations under the contract. [2] The Office of the Inspector General and the Board of Ethics cover employee ethics and fraud, not routine use-of-force complaints.

3 The District Attorney and criminal accountability

When an officer's conduct may be criminal, such as an on-duty shooting, the District Attorney decides whether to bring charges. The District Attorney's Office has a unit that reviews police-involved shootings and has prosecuted officers; it also maintains an internal process for tracking officers with credibility problems, which prosecutors must weigh under constitutional disclosure duties. District Attorney Larry Krasner was re-elected in November 2025. In 2022 the state House impeached him; on September 26, 2024 the Pennsylvania Supreme Court dismissed the matter on procedural grounds, holding that the articles lapsed with the end of the legislative session, without ruling on the allegations. [3] In the high-profile case of former officer Mark Dial, a jury in May 2025 convicted him of voluntary manslaughter (acquitting him of murder) and a judge sentenced him in July 2025; the District Attorney criticized the sentence as far below the guidelines. [4]

4 Arbitration and the union contract

Because police officers are public employees, discipline is shaped by Pennsylvania's collective bargaining laws and by the labor contract between the city and FOP Lodge 5. Dismissals and suspensions can be appealed to an arbitrator, whose decision is binding and reviewed by courts only narrowly. The effect, as described in The FOP Contract and Police Discipline, is that discipline can be reduced or reversed on appeal, a pattern that reform advocates criticize and that the union defends as due process. Police and fire contracts are set by interest arbitration under Act 111; see Public Sector Bargaining in Pennsylvania.

5 Consent decrees and federal review

A consent decree is a court-approved settlement that a judge can enforce. The most significant one for the department is in Bailey v. City of Philadelphia, a stop-and-frisk class action filed in 2010 by the ACLU of Pennsylvania and others. The 2011 decree requires the department to track stops, retrain and discipline officers when stops lack reasonable suspicion, and to report data to plaintiffs' counsel and a court-appointed monitor; advocates continue to publish reports on whether stops comply. [5] Unlike Chicago or Baltimore, Philadelphia has not been placed under a federal pattern-or-practice consent decree on policing generally. The U.S. Department of Justice has reviewed the department in other ways, including a 2015 assessment of deadly-force practices done at the city's request, and a 1979 DOJ lawsuit alleging police abuse that was dismissed in 1982 on standing grounds. The U.S. Attorney's Office can prosecute civil-rights crimes by officers.

6 Civil lawsuits and records

Individuals can sue officers and the city in state or federal court for damages. City settlement payouts are reported to City Council and the City Controller. Some records are available under the Right-to-Know Law, though investigative and personnel files carry exemptions. For steps to take, see How to File a Complaint and How to Report Corruption or Misconduct.
