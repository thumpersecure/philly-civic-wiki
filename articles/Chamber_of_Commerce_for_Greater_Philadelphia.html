The Chamber of Commerce for Greater Philadelphia is the region's principal business membership organization. It is a private nonprofit, not a government body, and has no regulatory authority; its influence comes from its membership of major employers, its advocacy before City Council, the mayor and the state legislature, and its convening role in regional economic development. 1 Structure and leadership

A board of directors drawn from member companies and institutions governs the Chamber, and a paid president and CEO runs day-to-day operations. Rob Wonderling, a former Pennsylvania state senator, led the Chamber from 2009 until he stepped down in mid-2022. He was succeeded by Chellie Cameron, who was named the Chamber's new president on May 19, 2022, after about eleven years as head of the City's aviation department and CEO of Philadelphia International Airport, according to the Inquirer and the Business Journals (see Philadelphia International Airport). The Inquirer reported on September 22, 2026, that the chief executive of Jefferson would chair the Chamber's board, a reminder that the board is drawn from the region's largest employers and institutions. As of 2026 Cameron remains president and CEO, and the Chamber publishes annual progress reports and messages to its members. [2]

2 Policy positions

The Chamber's public agenda has centered on business-tax competitiveness, workforce development, public safety, transportation and infrastructure, and regional economic growth. It has supported reductions in city wage and business taxes and has commented on proposals such as changes to the Business Income and Receipts Tax and the real-estate transfer tax (see Philadelphia Taxes). As with any advocacy group, its positions are those of its membership and can be disputed: labor unions, tenant groups and progressive organizations have at times opposed Chamber positions on wages, taxes and development incentives (see Tax Abatement and Development Incentives). [1]

3 Programs and affiliates

The Chamber runs networking and leadership programs, a regional leadership-exchange trip, small-business services and a political-engagement arm that endorses and hosts candidate forums in election years. In 2026 it worked with member institutions on visitor-welcome efforts tied to the nation's 250th anniversary; Temple University announced in July 2026 that it had joined the Chamber in welcoming visitors during the milestone year. It collaborates with the Philadelphia Industrial Development Corporation, the Commerce Department, and downtown organizations such as the Center City District. Its political-spending activity is subject to the rules described in Campaign Finance in Philadelphia. [2]

4 Place among business groups

The Chamber is one of several organized business voices. Others include the Building Industry Association and realtor groups (see Developers and the Real Estate Lobby), neighborhood commercial corridors, minority-business chambers, the Central Philadelphia Development Corporation, and sector associations in health care, hospitality and life sciences. Large anchor institutions also lobby on their own (see Eds and Meds Anchor Institutions).

5 How residents and small businesses interact with it

Membership is open to firms of any size for dues. Residents encounter the Chamber mainly through public testimony on tax bills, candidate forums and policy reports. Its filings and lobbying registrations are public; Pennsylvania lobbying disclosure and the City's lobbying rules are described in Conflicts of Interest and Pay-to-Play.
