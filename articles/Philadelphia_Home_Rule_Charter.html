The Philadelphia Home Rule Charter is the city's governing document, adopted by voters in 1951 as part of a mid-century "good government" reform movement aimed at ending patronage and machine control. It sets the structure of the executive branch and City Council, creates the independent offices and commissions, and sets rules for the budget, civil service and ethics. The 1951 Charter is still in force, though amended many times.[1]

1 Background

For much of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Philadelphia was governed under state-written legislation and dominated by a Republican organization. Reformers, including future mayors Joseph Clark and Richardson Dilworth, who won citywide office in 1949 as Democrats, campaigned against that arrangement. Pennsylvania voters approved a constitutional home rule amendment in 1922, and the legislature passed the First Class City Home Rule Act in 1949, allowing Philadelphia to draft its own charter. A charter commission produced the document, and voters approved it on April 17, 1951. It took effect with the new administration in January 1952.[2] The reform tradition also produced the Civil Service Commission and professionalized departments. For earlier context see Philadelphia Political History.

2 What the Charter establishes

The Charter creates a strong-mayor system. The mayor serves four-year terms and is limited to two consecutive terms; the mayor appoints most department heads. The Charter creates the position of Managing Director, the Finance Director and the City Solicitor (head of the Law Department); the Chief Administrative Officer sits alongside these in the mayor's team. It sets Council at 17 members, ten district and seven at large, and a rule that no voter may vote for more than five at-large candidates, so that a majority party can win at most five of the seven at-large seats and minority parties can win the other two. It names the elected row offices, requires an annual operating and capital budget (see Philadelphia City Budget Process), and provides for the Board of Ethics; the Inspector General is a related oversight office. It also contains the "resign-to-run" rule, which requires city officials to resign before running for another office.[3]

3 Amending the Charter

Charter changes require voter approval. Council typically places amendments on the ballot by ordinance. Dozens of amendments have added departments and duties over the decades, creating a document that Pew has described as long and prescriptive.[4] The resign-to-run rule has been the subject of repeated ballot-question debates, including in 2007 and 2014, according to WHYY coverage. In spring 2026, a Council committee removed a new resign-to-run proposal from the May ballot, according to press reports. See Ballot Questions and Charter Changes.

4 Limits on home rule

Home rule in Pennsylvania is not a grant of unlimited local power. The city may act on municipal matters unless the Constitution or an act of the General Assembly says otherwise. The state has repeatedly been held to preempt local laws on subjects such as firearms, and Philadelphia may impose only taxes the legislature has authorized. See Home Rule Limits and State Preemption and Pennsylvania State Government and Philadelphia.

5 Criticism and reform debates

Supporters credit the Charter with ending patronage-era corruption and providing stability. Critics, including some civic groups, argue that it has accumulated outdated detail, that Council's informal land-use customs sit awkwardly with it (see Councilmanic Prerogative), and that its structure divides accountability among the mayor, Council and independent offices. Proposals to convene a new charter review commission have been raised periodically.
