Transport Workers Union Local 234 (TWU 234) is the Philadelphia local of the Transport Workers Union of America. It represents bus, subway, trolley and other operating and maintenance employees of SEPTA, the regional transit authority. Because SEPTA is a state-created authority rather than a city agency, its labor relations are governed by Pennsylvania law and by federal transit labor rules rather than by the city's collective bargaining structure. Disputes involving TWU 234 nonetheless affect city commuters directly and often draw in the governor and the General Assembly.[1]

1 Union and employer

Will Vera is listed as the Local 234 representative on the executive board of the Philadelphia Council AFL-CIO. SEPTA was created by the Pennsylvania General Assembly on August 17, 1963 to coordinate government funding for transit and railroad companies in southeastern Pennsylvania, and began operating on February 18, 1964. It is governed by a 15-member board, most of whose members are appointed by the five counties it serves; two are appointed by the City of Philadelphia, one by the Mayor and one by the City Council President. Labor and fringe benefits made up about 71 percent of SEPTA's roughly $1.53 billion in operating expenses in fiscal 2021, which is why wage settlements are central to the authority's finances.[2]

SEPTA has a long record of stoppages: strikes occurred in 1977, 1981, 1983, 1986, 1995, 1998, 2005, 2009, 2014 and 2016. Not all involved TWU 234; the 1983 and 2014 stoppages, for example, were by Regional Rail unions. The authority's governing structure and its relationship with Philadelphia are described in SEPTA.[2]

2 The 2025 contract

SEPTA announced a tentative contract agreement with TWU 234, which it called its largest transit workers union, in December 2025, and the Philadelphia Inquirer reported on December 8, 2025 that a strike had been averted, and a later Inquirer report on December 18, 2025 covered the deal. Press accounts described a two-year agreement. The individual wage and benefit terms are set out in the agreement itself, which SEPTA and the union can provide; see Public Sector Bargaining in Pennsylvania for the legal framework that applies to public transit employees.[3]

3 Other SEPTA unions and the funding question

SEPTA also bargains with other unions, including those representing rail engineers and supervisors. SEPTA's wage settlements are tied to the authority's operating funding, which depends on state transit funding, local contributions and fare revenue; funding fights in Harrisburg are discussed in Pennsylvania State Government and Philadelphia. Bus drivers who work for private contractors, such as the school-transportation drivers who joined Teamsters Local 623 in 2024, are outside SEPTA's bargaining units.

4 Politics and public impact

Local 234 is affiliated with the Philadelphia Council AFL-CIO and has been a visible participant in campaigns on transit funding. Transit strikes affect schools, hospitals and businesses; residents can follow service alerts on SEPTA's own channels. See Office of Transportation, Infrastructure and Sustainability for the city's transportation role.
