The Philadelphia city budget process is the yearly cycle in which the Mayor proposes, City Council amends and adopts, and the state-created Pennsylvania Intergovernmental Cooperation Authority (PICA) reviews how the City will pay for its services. The City runs on a fiscal year that begins July 1, so a budget must be in place by the end of June. Because Philadelphia is a city and a county, its budget covers what other places split among municipal, county and sometimes school governments, although the School District of Philadelphia has its own budget and the City sends it local money.

1 Steps in the cycle

The Finance Department's Budget Office works with departments through the fall and winter to build proposals. The Mayor delivers a budget address to Council in March; Mayor Cherelle Parker gave the FY2027 address on March 12, 2026.[1] Council's Committee of the Whole then holds departmental hearings. In 2026 the Finance Director testified on March 25, the City Treasurer on March 24, and the CAO, Human Resources, Innovation and Technology, Public Property and Fleet Services departments on March 31.[2] Council members negotiate with the administration, changes are made in committee, and Council passes the operating and capital budget bills; the FY27 budgets received final passage on June 11, 2026.[3] The Mayor signed them into law, news reports said, within hours of the vote.[4] Tax bills, including rate changes, are separate ordinances needing votes of at least nine of Council's 17 members; see How a Bill Becomes Law in Philadelphia.

2 Operating, capital and the Five Year Plan

There are three linked documents. The operating budget pays for day-to-day services, salaries, benefits and debt service, mostly from the General Fund, with separate funds for water and sewer, the airport, grants and other purposes. The capital budget and six-year Capital Program finance buildings, streets, parks and water infrastructure, largely through borrowing; City Council's announcement of final passage described a $6.98 billion capital budget for FY27, alongside the FY27-32 Capital Program; capital figures describe borrowing and project authority over a multi-year program and are not comparable to the annual operating budget.[3] The Five Year Plan projects revenues and spending for the current and next four years and must be balanced; under the PICA Act, the City submits it to PICA, whose board votes on it. The PICA board approved the FY27-31 Plan unanimously on July 28, 2026.[6] The Mayor's Budget in Brief summarizes the adopted budget; see How to Read the City Budget.

3 The FY2027 budget

The adopted FY27 General Fund projects revenues of $6.578 billion, of which $4.84 billion (73.7 percent) comes from taxes and $797 million from the PICA portion of the resident wage tax; local non-tax revenue (fees, fines, permits) is projected at $435 million and state aid at $339 million. Expenditures total $7.13 billion, up $335.8 million (about 5 percent) from the FY26 estimate, driven by non-pension benefits, labor awards, a $91 million Federal Funding Reserve and deposits to a Labor Reserve (more than $605 million from FY26 to FY31).[5] New operating investments include $127.2 million for public safety, $51 million for economic opportunity and education, $12.1 million for housing and $9.2 million for clean and green initiatives, and the City's operating investment in SEPTA is $181 million.[5] Council dropped three of the Mayor's proposed revenue measures (a $1-per-trip ride-share tax, a hotel and short-term-rental tax and a retail delivery tax) after they failed to secure nine votes, and instead agreed to $48 million more for the School District and a new cell-tower use-and-occupancy tax change; see Philadelphia Taxes.[4]

4 Reserves and risks

The Plan estimates a fund balance of $364.6 million (5.5 percent of revenues) in FY27, falling to $59.1 million in FY29 as a legacy pension bond balloon payment lifts debt service by $64 million, then rising to $107.4 million at the end of the Plan. Including the Budget Stabilization Reserve (projected at $290 million in FY27) and the Federal Funding Reserve, total reserves are projected at $745.8 million in FY27, $495 million in FY29 and $543.2 million at the end; that meets the City's internal 6-8 percent target but is below the 17 percent recommended by the Government Finance Officers Association.[5] The budget document warns of risks from federal funding cuts (the City received over $2 billion in federal grants in FY25), the exhaustion of $1.4 billion in pandemic-era ARPA funds, and fringe benefits projected to rise 60.3 percent from FY25 to FY31 while total revenues grow 11.5 percent.[5]

5 How residents can take part

Residents can testify at Council's budget hearings and at public hearings on bills; see How to Testify at City Council. Organizations such as good-government groups publish budget analyses, and the independent City Controller reviews revenue estimates and audits spending. Budget documents and testimony are public; see Open Data and Transparency Tools.
