A business improvement district (BID), called a neighborhood improvement district in Pennsylvania law, is a defined area in which commercial property owners agree to pay an additional assessment collected by the city, which funds supplemental services run by a nonprofit. The largest and oldest in Philadelphia is the Center City District (CCD), which supplements city services in the downtown core. [1]

1 How BIDs work

Under state law and a City ordinance, property owners in a proposed district petition and vote, and the City then levies the approved assessment and passes it to the district's nonprofit. Services are meant to supplement, not replace, municipal ones: typical BID work includes sidewalk cleaning and trash removal, community-service ("ambassador") patrols, marketing, lighting, streetscape capital projects and data-driven planning. Districts must renew periodically by a process that gives owners a chance to object. Because the assessment is a charge on property rather than a tax on residents, residential-only blocks generally are not part of a BID. [1]

2 Center City District

CCD was created in the early 1990s by Center City property owners, with City approval, as an answer to downtown decline, and it has been renewed on multi-year plans. Paul Levy led it from its founding in 1991; Prema Katari Gupta became president and CEO on January 1, 2024, under a two-year leadership transition. CCD publishes plan-and-budget documents and an annual report that break down sources and uses of funds. Its work includes the downtown cleaning and safety teams, capital projects such as the Dilworth Park redevelopment and lighting and streetscape improvements, and research reports on downtown economics, office occupancy and retail. It has also published positions on business-tax reform. Its sister organization, the Central Philadelphia Development Corporation, handles planning and advocacy. [2]

3 Other BIDs

As of reporting, Philadelphia had about 15 BIDs. Examples include the University City District in West Philadelphia and the East Passyunk Avenue BID in South Philadelphia, and others serve Old City and commercial corridors elsewhere. The smallest have budgets of tens of thousands of dollars while the largest, CCD, operates at nearly $29 million. Smaller commercial corridors often rely instead on community development corporations and the City's corridor-management programs run through the Commerce Department. [3]

4 Influence and criticism

Supporters point to measurable improvements in cleanliness, foot traffic and downtown investment, and CCD is frequently cited in studies of BIDs nationally. Critics argue that BIDs concentrate public-space management in organizations governed by property owners rather than residents, that their patrols raise questions about oversight and treatment of people experiencing homelessness, and that wealthier districts can fund services unavailable in poorer neighborhoods. CCD and other BIDs respond that they supplement a baseline of City services and operate transparently under public plans. These disagreements often appear in debates over open streets, street cleaning and homeless outreach (see Office of Homeless Services).

5 Relationship to the City

BIDs have no police power; they depend on the Streets Department, the Philadelphia Police Department and other agencies for enforcement. Their leaders sit on commissions, testify before City Council and take part in zoning and design reviews (see Registered Community Organizations). Residents can contact a BID for cleaning requests in its district and use Philly311 for all other city services.
