The School District of Philadelphia is the public school system for the city of Philadelphia and the largest school district in Pennsylvania. It is a legally distinct local government from the City of Philadelphia: it has its own budget, its own board and its own employees, although it depends on the city for a large share of its local revenue and on the state for most of the rest. 1 Structure and governance

Policy is set by the nine-member Board of Education, which the mayor appoints from candidates recommended by an Educational Nominating Panel, with confirmation by City Council. The Board hires the superintendent, adopts the budget and approves major contracts, facilities decisions and charter-school actions. Tony Watlington has been superintendent since 2022; in September 2025 the Board voted to extend his contract through 2030, with annual raises of 3 percent on a salary then reported at about $367,700. [2]

The administration is organized into central-office divisions (academics, operations, finance, human resources, facilities) and neighborhood-based "learning networks" that supervise principals. Teachers are represented by the Philadelphia Federation of Teachers, and school-based support staff and building engineers are represented by other unions. The District is subject to the Pennsylvania Public School Code, the Sunshine Act and the Right-to-Know Law. Before 2018 the District was governed by the state-dominated School Reform Commission, created in 2001; the SRC voted to dissolve in November 2017 and the appointed Board replaced it in July 2018. [1]

2 Students and schools

According to the District's research office, 198,405 students were enrolled in Philadelphia public schools as of October 1, 2025, a net increase of 106 over the previous year. Of that total, 64,469 attended charter schools, which are publicly funded but independently operated. The District has reported a 12 percent enrollment decline between 2014-15 and 2024-25, driven in part by the growth of charters and by population changes. [3]

District-run schools include neighborhood schools, citywide-admission and special-admission high schools, and alternative programs. Students living within a school's catchment have a right to attend; many high schools admit by application.

3 Money and the budget

The Board approved a roughly $4.6 billion operating budget for 2026-27. The District has described a structural deficit of about $300 million and, in the plan advanced by Superintendent Watlington, ordered about $225 million in reductions: about $56 million in school-level changes (including the elimination of roughly 340 teaching, counseling and climate positions) and about $169 million from the central office and lower-return programs, as reported by local education press. [4]

Revenue comes from the state (basic education funding and the adequacy supplement that followed the 2023 Commonwealth Court school-funding ruling), local sources (chiefly the school share of the city property tax, plus other local taxes), and federal programs. City Council approves the school property-tax rate but cannot direct how the Board spends funds. The budget process interacts with the City budget process and with tax policy described in Philadelphia Taxes.

4 Facilities plan and school closures

The District operates some of the oldest school buildings in the country. The Board has approved a ten-year facilities plan estimated at about $3 billion that calls for closing 17 schools beginning with the 2027-28 school year (the Board approved the plan on April 30, 2026), modernizing about 169 campuses and co-locating six others. Closure proposals were contested by some families, teachers and elected officials, and the plan's roll-out has generated ongoing public debate over enrollment projections, building conditions and neighborhood impact. [5]

5 Who holds influence

Formal authority sits with the Board, the superintendent and the mayor (who appoints the Board and sets city-side funding priorities). The state legislature and the Governor control basic-education appropriations and the Charter School Law. The teachers' union is the District's largest organized constituency. Major philanthropies, higher-education partners and business groups such as the Chamber of Commerce for Greater Philadelphia participate through advisory roles and grants (see Philanthropy in Philadelphia). Parents and neighborhood groups influence decisions through public testimony, school advisory councils and, increasingly, organized opposition to closures.

6 Working with the District

Residents can speak at Board meetings by signing up in advance, request records through the Right-to-Know process, and raise school-level concerns with the principal, then the learning network, then the Office of Family and Community Engagement. See also How to File a Complaint.
