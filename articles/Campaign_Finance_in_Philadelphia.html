Campaign finance in Philadelphia is governed by city law that caps contributions to candidates for city office, requires periodic disclosure, and lets the Board of Ethics enforce the rules through fines and, in the most serious cases, court action. Pennsylvania state law, by contrast, has no contribution limits for state candidates, so Philadelphia's limits are among the strictest in the state. Contribution limits are adjusted every four years for inflation; the figures below reflect the adjustment certified for January 1, 2024, the most recent quadrennial adjustment, and the Board's Regulation No. 1 should be checked for any later change.[1]

1 Contribution limits

Under the city law, an individual may give up to $3,700 per calendar year to a candidate's political committee, a litigation fund committee or a transition and inauguration committee; political committees, business organizations and other non-individuals may give up to $14,800, according to the Board of Ethics' February 2024 advisory. If a candidate gives personal funds above the threshold set in the law, the limits double for all candidates for that office, to $7,400 and $29,600 respectively under the 2024 figures. The Board of Ethics issues advisories when the doubling is triggered, as it did for the 2023 mayor's race (January 2023) and the 2023 Council at-large race (May 2023).[2] Candidates may spend unlimited personal funds, a consequence of U.S. Supreme Court precedent.

2 Reporting and disclosure

Candidate committees file periodic reports listing contributions and expenditures; reports become public through the city's election offices and the Board of Ethics. Candidates and officials also file annual statements of financial interests. For state and federal offices, reports are filed with the Pennsylvania Department of State and Federal Election Commission. See Running for Office in Philadelphia.

3 Independent expenditures

Outside groups, including super PACs, can raise and spend unlimited sums to support or oppose candidates as long as they do not coordinate with the candidate. Coordination, if proven, converts such spending into contributions subject to the limits. The Inquirer reported on September 16, 2026 that political parties can now spend unlimited amounts in coordination with their candidates. See Political Action Committees and Big Money.

4 The 2023 mayoral race

The 2023 Democratic mayoral primary was the most expensive in city history. The Inquirer reported that Democratic candidates and the independent groups supporting them raised more than $34.6 million and spent about $37.7 million, with super PACs accounting for nearly $5 million; Allan Domb gave more than $11 million of his own money and Jeff Brown $4 million.[3] Cherelle Parker won the primary.

5 Enforcement and debate

The Board of Ethics settles most violations by negotiated agreement and fine. In April 2023 the Board sued a super PAC supporting mayoral candidate Jeff Brown, alleging coordination to circumvent the limits. In September 2023 a judge ruled against the Board, according to the Inquirer, and in May 2024 the Board updated its campaign finance regulation.[4] The defendants denied wrongdoing. Supporters of strict limits say they reduce the influence of large donors; critics argue limits push money into outside groups and favor wealthy self-funders. See also Conflicts of Interest and Pay-to-Play.
