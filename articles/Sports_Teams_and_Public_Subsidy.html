Sports Teams and Public Subsidy
TeamsPhiladelphia Phillies (MLB), Philadelphia Eagles (NFL), Philadelphia 76ers (NBA), Philadelphia Flyers (NHL)
LocationSouth Philadelphia Sports Complex
Arena projectNew Sixers-Flyers arena on the former Spectrum site, targeted to open in 2030, described by the teams as fully privately funded
OwnersHarris Blitzer Sports and Entertainment (Sixers); Comcast Spectacor (Flyers)
Public bodies involvedCity Council, Planning Commission, Zoning Board, PIDC, state legislature

Sports teams and public subsidy refers to the debate over how much public money, land and regulatory support professional teams should receive. Philadelphia is unusual in having all four major league franchises clustered at one South Philadelphia site, so decisions about leases, stadium financing and event-day services recur at City Hall and in Harrisburg. 1 The Sports Complex

The Phillies (Citizens Bank Park), the Eagles (Lincoln Financial Field), and the Sixers and Flyers (the arena currently on site) play on land that the City owns or controls, much of which is leased to the teams. Lincoln Financial Field (2003) and Citizens Bank Park (2004) opened with a mix of public and private funds from city and state sources. Sports-related public services include event-day policing and traffic management by the Philadelphia Police Department, Streets Department and SEPTA. Teams are large taxpayers through wage, amusement and other taxes (see Philadelphia Taxes).

2 The failed Market East arena plan

In 2022 the 76ers, through a development company called 76 Devcorp, unveiled plans for a roughly $1.3 billion arena on the Fashion District site in Center City, targeted for the 2031-32 season. The plan met strong opposition from Chinatown residents and organizations concerned about displacement and traffic, and in January 2025 the team abandoned it. [2]

3 The South Philadelphia arena

In January 2025 Harris Blitzer Sports and Entertainment and Comcast Spectacor announced that they would jointly build a new arena in the South Philadelphia complex to house both the Sixers and the Flyers, targeted for 2031 or sooner. By September 2026 the teams released first renderings and said the arena, designed by Populous and Moody Nolan, would open in 2030 ahead of a WNBA expansion team's first season. They describe it as the largest privately funded project in city history, with no public contribution to construction, and project nearly 15,000 jobs and almost $6 billion in economic activity over ten years, including tax revenue for the city and the School District; those projections are the teams' own. The partnership also pledged to invest in the Market East area. Some regulatory approvals, lease and land arrangements, and public-service commitments remain to be reviewed by public bodies. [3]

4 Debates over subsidies

Supporters of public support argue that teams generate jobs, tourism and civic identity. Economists who study stadium finance have often found limited net economic benefit relative to public cost, and critics, including journalists who track stadium deals, argue that infrastructure support, tax breaks or leases at low rent can function as indirect subsidies even when construction is private. Questions often asked about the South Philadelphia project include whether it benefits from tax abatement and development incentives, who pays for infrastructure and event services, and how the project affects nearby residents. The teams say the project will not rely on public construction funds.

5 Who holds influence

Team owners and Comcast hold large business and political relationships; City Council, the mayor and district Council members hold land-use and legislative leverage; the state legislature controls state-level incentives. Building trades unions typically support construction projects (see Philadelphia Building and Construction Trades Council), while neighborhood and Chinatown groups were pivotal in the Market East outcome (see Civic and Neighborhood Associations).
