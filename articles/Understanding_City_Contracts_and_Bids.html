City contracts are how Philadelphia converts budget dollars into goods, services and construction, from road paving and software to legal services and food for shelters. The rules aim at competition, price, quality, fairness and local economic opportunity. This guide explains the system as of 2026 for residents, vendors and watchdogs. Thresholds and goals can change; confirm on phila.gov.[1]

1 Who does what

The Procurement Department runs bidding and contracting for supplies, equipment and services. Public works (construction) contracts are managed with the Department of Public Property and agencies such as the Streets Department and Water Department; see Department of Public Property. The Law Department reviews contracts as to form. The Office of Economic Opportunity sets and monitors participation goals for minority-, woman- and disabled-owned businesses. The Finance Department and City Controller handle payment authorization and audits. Authorities such as PHA, SEPTA, the School District and PIDC have their own procurement.

2 Types of contracts

  • Public works: construction and repair, usually requiring prevailing wages, bonds and insurance; see Prevailing Wage and Project Labor Agreements.
  • Supplies, equipment and non-professional services: competitively bid.
  • Professional services: legal, engineering, consulting; typically selected by qualifications through a request for proposals (RFP) rather than lowest price, with a selection committee.
  • Small purchases: informal quotes below the formal threshold.
  • Sole-source or emergency contracts: allowed in limited circumstances with justification; these draw the most scrutiny.
  • Grants and leases are different instruments but also public money; see Department of Public Property.

3 How a competitive bid works

  1. The department defines needs and the Procurement Department posts an invitation for bids on PHLContracts.
  2. Vendors register an account, complete the required disclosures and consent agreements, and submit sealed bids by the closing date. Requirements include insurance, bonds or bid security where applicable, wage and labor compliance, and city contract terms.
  3. Bids are opened, and for formal bids the contract goes to the lowest responsive and responsible bidder (not simply the cheapest if the bidder is unqualified or the bid is non-compliant).
  4. For RFPs, evaluators score technical and cost proposals, interview finalists and recommend an award.
  5. The contract is executed after legal review and, where required, ordinance authority or board approval. Formal contracts at or above $34,000 are the competitive sealed bid category per city guidance.
  6. Payments follow vouchers and performance, with oversight from the Controller.

4 Diversity, local preference, and wages

The city has a stated goal of directing at least 35 percent of for-profit contract dollars to minority-, woman- and disabled-owned business enterprises (M/W/DSBEs), which register with the Office of Economic Opportunity. Bidders must submit participation plans, and the city may give consideration to Local Business Entities under local bidding preference regulations. Compliance is monitored; the OEO Compliance Hotline (215-683-1798, free and anonymous, 24 hours) takes reports of non-compliance. Critics and audits have at times questioned how well goals are met, and the Controller and press have reported on them; evaluate such claims from the primary reports. Workers on city projects may be covered by prevailing wage and the city's labor standards. See Office of Worker Protections and Philadelphia Building and Construction Trades Council.[2]

5 Approvals and money

Contracts come from budgeted funds; see How to Read the City Budget. Large or long-term arrangements, land sales and some development deals require ordinances passed by City Council (see How a Bill Becomes Law in Philadelphia). Pay-to-play restrictions limit contributions by contractors; see Conflicts of Interest and Pay-to-Play and Campaign Finance in Philadelphia. Employees and officials are subject to the ethics code enforced by the Board of Ethics.

6 How residents can follow and report

  • Search open bids and awards on PHLContracts and the city's open data portal: Open Data and Transparency Tools.
  • Read Council ordinance texts and hearing records for large contracts.
  • Request contract documents, scoring sheets and invoices: How to Request Public Records.
  • Report suspected fraud or waste to the Inspector General or Controller: How to Report Corruption or Misconduct.
  • Small businesses seeking work should contact the Procurement Department, OEO and the Commerce Department.
