The Office of Economic Opportunity (OEO) works "to support fair and open access to City contracting opportunities." It promotes transparency in procurement, monitors compliance with City contract requirements and helps businesses navigate participation through workshops and resources.[1] The City's current web page lists OEO as a division of the Office of Business Impact and Economic Advancement, an economic-development office that works alongside the Department of Commerce, and OEO ensures that the City is working with diverse businesses to fulfill its needs for goods and services. In practice it is the office that registers minority-owned, woman-owned and disabled-owned business enterprises (M/W/DSBEs), reviews and monitors City contracts for compliance with participation commitments, and supports businesses that want to become prime contractors.[2]

1 The registry and certification

To join the OEO Registry, a business must first be certified by an approved outside agency. The City accepts certifications from the National Minority Supplier Development Council (NMSDC), the Women's Business Enterprise Council (WBEC), the Women's Business Enterprise National Council (WBENC), the Pennsylvania Unified Certification Program and federal or regional transit authorities.[1] To qualify, a business must be at least 51 percent owned by a minority, a woman or a person with a disability.[2] Businesses use an online system to apply for registration, renew and see outreach opportunities. A public list of registered firms is available as an open dataset (see Open Data and Transparency Tools).

2 Participation goals and how they work

Each year the City aims for 35 percent participation by M/W/DSBEs on its contracts.[2] Bid solicitations from the Procurement Department and other agencies may set ranges of participation for minority-, woman- and disabled-owned firms, and bidders must show a good-faith plan, often by naming subcontractors. OEO reviews those plans and monitors the contract. Department testimony to City Council reports results: the Finance Department reported M/W/DSBE participation of 31 percent on $26.4 million of professional-services contracts in FY25, and the Revenue Department 34 percent on $20.1 million.[3] Because contract data differ by department, aggregate results require OEO annual reports. The City is also building a registry of small and local businesses (S/LBEs) with department-level goals, in the range of 25 to 40 percent in 2026 testimony.[3] The rules apply to City contracts and not to state or federal contracts, which have their own programs. See Understanding City Contracts and Bids.

3 Programs and workshops

OEO runs "Doing Business with the City" and "Business Development Series" workshops that teach firms how to find bids, price work and meet insurance and compliance requirements. Its capacity-building staff help small firms scale to become prime contractors. It also receives complaints about fraud and waste on contracts (the OEO page directs the public to a fraud and waste reporting channel).[1] OEO's nine listed staff include business enterprise specialists and coordinators. Its work overlaps with the Commerce Department's small-business services (see Commerce Department) and with the Philadelphia Industrial Development Corporation lending programs, and with trade-labor requirements on public projects (see Prevailing Wage and Project Labor Agreements).

4 Related initiatives and reorganization

In March 2025 Mayor Cherelle Parker named Nadir Jones as Director of Business Impact and Supplier Diversity within an Office of Business Impact and Economic Advancement.[4] The City's OEO web page now places OEO within that Office of Business Impact and Economic Advancement and lists a deputy director as its leader; the page does not describe the reorganization, so residents with questions about who handles a particular registry or compliance matter should call the OEO number above. The Mayor's FY27 budget also added an Economic Mobility Cabinet and an Office of Financial Empowerment, which aim to widen access to economic opportunity.[5] The earlier history of M/W/DSBE contracting in Philadelphia, including Council legislation, is outside the scope of the sources consulted.

5 Reporting problems

Businesses and workers who believe a contractor is violating participation commitments can contact OEO at the number above. Suspected fraud can also be reported to the Office of the Inspector General and the City Controller; see How to Report Corruption or Misconduct. Contract-related ethics rules are enforced by the Board of Ethics. Community groups that support local business include community development corporations and the Chamber of Commerce for Greater Philadelphia.
