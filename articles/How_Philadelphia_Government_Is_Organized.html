Philadelphia's government is best understood as several systems operating side by side: the mayor's departments, the legislature, independently elected officials, the state-run court system, state-created authorities, and layers of state and federal law. 1 The executive branch

The mayor heads the executive branch and appoints most department heads. Day-to-day operations of many line departments are overseen by the Managing Director, a position that changed hands in July 2026 when Adam Thiel's departure and the naming of Carlton Williams as his successor were announced.[1] Central support functions include the Finance Department, the Law Department, the Office of Human Resources, Procurement, and the Chief Administrative Officer. The full list is in Mayor's Cabinet and Offices. Service departments include public safety (Police, Fire, Prisons), health and human services (Public Health, Human Services), and land and infrastructure (Licenses and Inspections, Streets, Water, Parks and Recreation).

2 The legislative branch

City Council passes ordinances, approves the operating and capital budgets, and conducts oversight hearings. Its leadership and procedures are described in Council Committees and the Legislative Process. Council can override a mayoral veto by a two-thirds vote. In practice, district members also exercise informal control over land-use decisions in their districts (see Councilmanic Prerogative).

The annual budget shows how the pieces fit together. The fiscal year runs from July 1 to June 30. In March the mayor presents a proposed budget to Council in a budget address; for fiscal year 2027, Mayor Cherelle Parker proposed a $6.97 billion budget on March 12, 2026. Council holds hearings with each department and elected office and then votes, and for FY2027 Council approved the operating and capital budgets on June 11, 2026.[4] The School District has its own separate budget, adopted by the Board of Education (see Philadelphia Board of Education). More detail is in Philadelphia City Budget Process.

Philadelphia is both a city and a county. Since the 1854 consolidation, one government has performed both roles, and the 1951 Home Rule Charter, effective in January 1952, folded most county functions into city government while leaving several county offices as independently elected row offices (see City-County Consolidation). Residents therefore deal with four layers at once: the city, the commonwealth, the federal government and independent bodies such as the School District and state-created authorities.

3 Elected row offices

Five offices are elected separately and are not under the mayor's control: the Controller (auditor), the District Attorney (prosecutor), the Sheriff, the City Commissioners (who administer elections), and the Register of Wills. See Philadelphia Row Offices. The personnel system is policed by the Civil Service Commission.

4 Boards, commissions and authorities

Dozens of boards advise or regulate. Land-use examples include the City Planning Commission, the Zoning Board of Adjustment, and the Historical Commission. Ethics and oversight are handled by the Board of Ethics and the Inspector General. Independent public authorities created under state law, with their own boards and budgets, include SEPTA, the Philadelphia Housing Authority, the Redevelopment Authority, and the Parking Authority. The mayor typically appoints some or all board members, but the bodies are legally distinct from city departments.

5 Courts, state and federal layers

Philadelphia's trial courts, the Court of Common Pleas and Municipal Court, form the First Judicial District and are part of Pennsylvania's state judiciary; judges are elected (see Court of Common Pleas and Municipal Court). The commonwealth shapes city powers through preemption, taxing authority and oversight bodies like PICA (see Home Rule Limits and State Preemption and Pennsylvania State Government and Philadelphia). Federal grants, courts and law enforcement are covered in Federal Government and Philadelphia.
