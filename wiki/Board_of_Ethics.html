Board of Ethics

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
Board of Ethics
TypeIndependent city board (created by the Home Rule Charter)
Executive DirectorJ. Shane Creamer Jr., in the post since 2007
Established2006 Charter amendment; operating since 2007
AddressOne Parkway Building, 18th Floor, 1515 Arch Street, Philadelphia
Governing lawHome Rule Charter; Philadelphia Code Chapters 20-600 (public integrity) and 17-1400 (campaign finance)
Websitephila.gov/departments/board-of-ethics

The Board of Ethics is the independent city body that administers and enforces Philadelphia's ethics, campaign finance, lobbying and financial-disclosure rules for city officials, employees and candidates. It is a city-charter body, separate from the state-level Pennsylvania State Ethics Commission and from the law-enforcement role of the District Attorney and federal prosecutors. It was created after voters approved a Charter amendment in 2006, in a period of public concern about public integrity.[1]

1 Structure

The Board has five members, appointed by the mayor and approved by City Council, who serve five-year terms, according to the city's website. A professional staff is led by an executive director. J. Shane Creamer Jr. has led the staff since 2007, according to the Board's website.[2] Staff handle investigations, legal advice, campaign finance review, training and enforcement. The Board's budget is part of the city budget set by Council; see Philadelphia City Budget Process.

2 Responsibilities

  • Public integrity: rules on gifts, conflicts of interest, nepotism, post-employment limits and use of public resources for political activity.
  • Campaign finance: contribution limits, reporting and disclosure; see Campaign Finance in Philadelphia.
  • Lobbying: registration and reporting by people paid to influence city decisions.
  • Financial disclosure: annual statements of financial interests for officials, which are public.
  • Advice and training: the Board issues advisory opinions, regulations and trainings for city staff and candidates.

3 Complaints and enforcement

Anyone may file a complaint, anonymously or by name. Staff review it, and if they find a likely violation they may negotiate a settlement agreement approved by the Board, which typically includes a fine. The Board can also go to court to enforce rules. Settlements and advisory opinions are posted on the Board's website. The Board does not prosecute crimes; criminal matters go to the District Attorney, the U.S. Attorney's Office or other authorities. Employee misconduct within mayor-controlled agencies is also investigated by the Office of the Inspector General. For a how-to, see How to Report Corruption or Misconduct.

4 Notable disputes

In April 2023 the Board sued a super PAC supporting mayoral candidate Jeff Brown, alleging the group coordinated with his campaign to exceed limits. A judge ruled against the Board in September 2023, according to the Inquirer, and in May 2024 the Board updated its campaign finance regulation. The respondents denied violating the law.[3] In an earlier episode in 2009 the Board fined its own executive director a small sum for a minor violation, as reported by the Inquirer. Elected officials and the Board have also disagreed publicly over the boundaries between the Board's authority and that of the Inspector General.

5 Other watchdogs

The Board is one of several oversight bodies: the City Controller audits finances, the Inspector General investigates fraud and misconduct, and private groups monitor government; see Good Government Groups. For state-level rules, the Pennsylvania Public Official and Employee Ethics Act applies to city officials in some respects, such as financial-interest statements.
