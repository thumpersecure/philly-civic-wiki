Democratic City Committee

The Democratic City Committee (also called the Philadelphia Democratic Party or the Democratic Executive Committee) is the local organization of the Democratic Party in Philadelphia. Because Democrats hold a large majority of the city's registered voters, the committee is often described as the most influential party organization in the city. It is a private political association, not a city agency, but its ward structure is recognized by the Pennsylvania Election Code.[1]

1 Structure and leadership

The committee is built from the bottom up. Registered Democrats in each of the city's roughly 1,700 voting divisions elect two committeepeople every four years; the committeepeople in each of the 66 wards then elect a ward leader; and the ward leaders elect the citywide chair. Details are in Ward Leaders and Committeepersons. The party's website lists a chair, vice chairs, treasurer and staff, including an executive director.[2] Bob Brady, a former U.S. representative, has been chair since 1986.

2 Functions

The committee endorses candidates before primaries (its endorsement is influential but not binding), raises money, maintains voter-contact networks, and coordinates Election Day staffing. Ward leaders often help select candidates for judicial vacancies and special elections, and in some circumstances party committees choose replacement nominees under state law. The committee works alongside labor unions and elected officials; see Labor and Politics in Philadelphia. Party finances are subject to campaign finance reporting; see Campaign Finance in Philadelphia.

3 The 2026 elections

The May 19, 2026 primary included elections for ward committee seats. The Philadelphia Citizen reported that at least 1,018 of the 3,406 ward committee seats would be filled by newcomers, about 30 percent, and highlighted organized challenges by the progressive group Reclaim Philadelphia in several wards.[3] In June 2026 ward leaders re-elected Brady to another four-year term; the Inquirer reported that no challenger stepped forward, while left-leaning candidates had won at least five ward leader posts the week before.[4] After the primary, District Attorney Larry Krasner publicly called on Brady to resign, a call Brady did not heed according to the same reporting.

4 Criticism and reform efforts

Critics, including progressive groups and some good-government advocates, argue that low turnout in committee elections and long-serving ward leaders make the organization difficult to change; supporters respond that the committee provides neighborhood-level organizing and voter turnout that other groups lack. Several reform efforts focus on filling vacant committee seats with younger and more diverse members and on competing for ward leadership. See Progressive and Grassroots Politics.

5 Getting involved

Any registered Democrat can run for committee person in their division by filing a petition in the primary year, or can win a seat by write-in votes; the City Commissioners publish write-in results after each primary. Vacancies are filled by the ward committee. To find your ward and division, use How to Find Your Elected Officials.
