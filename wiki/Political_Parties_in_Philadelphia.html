Political Parties in Philadelphia

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
Political Parties in Philadelphia
TypeOverview
Major partiesDemocratic (large majority of registered voters); Republican
Other active partiesWorking Families Party, Green, Libertarian, others
Primary typeClosed (party-registered voters only for partisan races)
Registered votersAbout 1.08 million (2026 primary)
Governing lawPennsylvania Election Code; Philadelphia Home Rule Charter

Political parties in Philadelphia operate within a system in which the Democratic Party holds a large registration advantage, the Republican Party competes mainly in parts of the Northeast and River Wards, and minor parties play a limited but increasingly visible role. Because the Democratic primary effectively decides most local general elections, the real contest for most offices occurs inside the Democratic Party. Pennsylvania uses closed primaries, so only voters registered with a party may vote in that party's primary for partisan offices.[1]

1 Registration and the primary

As of the May 2026 primary the city had about 1.08 million registered voters, according to the City Commissioners' election results.[2] Democrats make up a large majority of registrants. Voters registered as Independent or with a minor party cannot vote in the Democratic or Republican primaries for candidates, although they may vote on ballot questions. Candidates for city office run in a May primary held in odd-numbered years; general elections follow in November. See Elections in Philadelphia.

2 The major parties

The Democratic City Committee, led since 1986 by Bob Brady, is the largest local party organization. The Republican City Committee is far smaller but retains ward organizations, particularly in the Northeast. Both parties are formally organized by the 66 wards and committeepeople described in Ward Leaders and Committeepersons. Parties endorse candidates, circulate petitions, staff polling places and distribute "street sheets" or sample ballots, but under state law the party cannot place a candidate on the ballot without voters' nominating petitions or primary victory.

3 Minor parties and minority representation

The Home Rule Charter reserves two of Council's seven at-large seats for minority-party candidates by limiting how many at-large candidates a single party can elect. Republicans traditionally held these seats; in 2019 Kendra Brooks of the Working Families Party became the first third-party candidate to win one, and the party has since held two seats. See Philadelphia City Council and Progressive and Grassroots Politics. Ranked-choice voting is not used in city elections.

4 Party structure

Each party's local committee is a private organization governed by its own rules and the state Election Code. Its members are elected every four years at the primary. Party chairs are elected by ward leaders. State parties (Pennsylvania Democratic Party, Republican Party of Pennsylvania) sit above the city committees; as of 2026, state Sen. Sharif Street chairs the state Democratic Party.[3] Philadelphia's large Democratic delegation gives the city committee influence over state-level nominations. Campaign money flowing through parties and committees is discussed in Campaign Finance in Philadelphia.
