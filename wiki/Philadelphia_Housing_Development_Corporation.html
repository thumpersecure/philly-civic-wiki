Philadelphia Housing Development Corporation

Philadelphia Housing Development Corporation
TypeNonprofit corporation closely tied to the City; implementing agency for housing programs

The Philadelphia Housing Development Corporation (PHDC) is a nonprofit that carries out many of Philadelphia's housing programs, including home repair and land transactions. It is neither a city department nor a state authority: it is a private nonprofit corporation that works under agreements with the City and with the two public land bodies it staffs. Understanding PHDC is useful because residents who apply for home repair help or ask about a vacant lot are often dealing with PHDC staff even when the legal decision-maker is another body.[1]

1 Role

PHDC acts as an implementing partner for the City's housing policy. The policy and funding come from the mayor's office, City Council and the Division of Housing and Community Development; PHDC administers programs, processes applications, manages construction and repair work through contractors, and handles property transactions. Using a nonprofit gives the City flexibility to hire specialized staff, take on loans and move faster than some municipal processes allow, but it also means that the corporation is not directly covered by every rule that applies to city departments, such as the civil service system.

2 The 2019 consolidation

Bisnow Philadelphia reported that the Land Bank and the Redevelopment Authority combined their duties and staffs into PHDC in a reorganization announced in 2019. The two public boards remained intact, and the staff transferred through memoranda of understanding with support from Council. PHDC's President and CEO David Thomas kept his position; Angel Rodriguez, formerly head of the Land Bank, became senior vice president of land management; and Gregory Heller, former executive director of the Redevelopment Authority, became senior vice president of community investment. Thomas said no jobs were lost and no office space was added or vacated.[2] The purpose, officials said, was a "one-stop shop" for how Philadelphians engage with the City's acquisition, disposition and development of public land. A bill was then pending to transfer the functions of the Vacant Property Review Committee to the Land Bank.

3 Programs

  • Home repair. PHDC administers programs for low- and moderate-income homeowners, including the Basic Systems Repair Program for emergency system failures and loan programs for larger repairs, funded mainly through DHCD.[1]
  • Land management. Staff handle applications for vacant lots and buildings held by the Land Bank and the Redevelopment Authority, including side-yard sales and Turn the Key developer sales.
  • Community investment. PHDC coordinates funding and technical help for community development corporations and commercial corridors (see Community Development Corporations).
  • Real estate development. The corporation can partner with developers on affordable housing projects and manage city-owned property.

Under the mayor's 2025 H.O.M.E. plan to create and preserve 30,000 homes, PHDC's land and repair functions are central to delivery.[3] Specific figures on current PHDC budget and staffing should be taken from its annual reports and the City's budget documents.

4 Accountability

Because PHDC spends public funds, it is subject to audits by funders and the City Controller, and the public bodies it staffs are subject to the Sunshine Act and Right-to-Know Law. Critics and reporters have at times examined the pace of vacant land transfers and the size of home-repair waiting lists; Bisnow reported, for example, that fiscal-year transfers in 2022 picked up but fell short of goals.[4] Supporters note that demand for repairs and land consistently exceeds available funding.

5 Contacting PHDC

Residents seeking repair help usually begin with DHCD or PHDC application pages and may be referred to partner agencies. For questions about whether a lot is publicly owned, see the Land Bank article and the City's open data tools. Complaints about a contractor or service can be raised with PHDC directly, then with DHCD, and as a last step through the City's complaint channels.
