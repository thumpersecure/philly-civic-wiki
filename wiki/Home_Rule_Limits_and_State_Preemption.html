Home Rule Limits and State Preemption

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
Home rule limits and state preemption

Home rule limits and state preemption describe the ways Pennsylvania law narrows what Philadelphia can do under its Home Rule Charter. The city may govern itself on local matters, but the General Assembly can override local ordinances by statute, and the courts enforce that hierarchy.[1]

1 The basic rule

Pennsylvania's Constitution lets home rule municipalities exercise any power not denied by the Constitution, their charter or the General Assembly. Philadelphia is governed by a special statute, the First Class City Home Rule Act of 1949, which lists powers the city cannot exercise without state authorization. In effect, a local ordinance is valid unless a state law forbids it or occupies the field. Courts distinguish between "express" preemption, where a statute says so, and "implied" or field preemption, where state law is so comprehensive that no room remains for local rules. Disputes are litigated in the Commonwealth Court, with appeals to the Pennsylvania Supreme Court.

2 Taxes

The city's tax power is the most important limit. Philadelphia may levy only taxes the legislature has authorized. Its wage and business taxes, for example, rest on enabling acts such as the 1932 Sterling Act, and the beverage tax Council adopted in 2016 was challenged as exceeding that authority; the state Supreme Court upheld it in 2018.[2] In March 2026 Mayor Cherelle Parker proposed a $1-per-ride tax on ride-hailing services to help fund the School District, according to CBS News. Whether the city can create a tax often depends on whether state law already covers the subject. See Philadelphia Taxes.

3 Firearms

State law bars municipalities from regulating the ownership, possession, transfer or transportation of firearms. Philadelphia has repeatedly tried to adopt stricter local gun laws and has been repeatedly stopped by the courts. On November 20, 2024, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court ruled 6-0 in a suit brought by the city, relatives of gun violence victims and CeaseFirePA that the preemption laws stand, with Justice Kevin Brobson writing that there was nothing for the court to do absent a constitutional violation. The court said the remedy is for the legislature.[3]

4 Other fields

State law also sets or limits local action on landlord-tenant rules, minimum wage and paid leave in some respects, labor relations (see Public Sector Bargaining in Pennsylvania), school governance and funding (see School District of Philadelphia), alcohol and gaming, and authorities such as SEPTA and the Parking Authority. The legislature has also imposed financial oversight on the city through PICA. Where Philadelphia has acted, as with local worker-protection and housing laws, the extent of its authority can be contested in court.

5 Working within the limits

When it cannot legislate directly, the city lobbies Harrisburg through the mayor's intergovernmental office, uses executive orders, funds local programs, or sues. Council sometimes passes resolutions urging the state to act. The politics of preemption is partly partisan: Philadelphia is heavily Democratic, while the legislature has included Republican majorities in one or both chambers. See Pennsylvania State Government and Philadelphia.
