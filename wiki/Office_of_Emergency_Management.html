The Office of Emergency Management (OEM) is the City of Philadelphia's coordinating agency for emergencies and major events. It does not itself fight fires or make arrests. Instead it plans for hazards, activates the city's Emergency Operations Center (EOC) when needed, and brings together the Police, Fire, Streets, Water, Public Health and other departments, along with the state, federal agencies, utilities, SEPTA and community partners. [1]

1 Legal basis and structure

Pennsylvania's Emergency Management Services Code requires each county, including the City and County of Philadelphia, to maintain an emergency management agency and a county emergency operations plan.[5] In Philadelphia that function sits in OEM, which is organized within the Managing Director's Office. The director is appointed by the Mayor. The city's website lists Dominick Mireles as director; he was appointed in April 2022, after more than a decade with OEM, where he had been Deputy Director for Operations, and he also serves as the city's Deputy Director of Community Safety. [2] The office's history page dates the first emergency-management organization to 1941; in 1972 the Civil Defense Council became the Office of Emergency Preparedness within the Fire Department, and in November 1985 it moved to the Managing Director's Office. The Mayor has authority to declare a local disaster emergency under state law, which can unlock additional powers such as emergency purchasing and requests for state and federal assistance.

2 What OEM does

OEM's work falls into four categories: preparedness (maintaining the Emergency Operations Plan, training, exercises and the city's Hazard Mitigation Plan), response (running the EOC and coordinating multi-agency operations), recovery (assisting with damage assessments and requests for disaster assistance), and public information (alerts and outreach). It also manages the ReadyPhiladelphia text and email alert program, issues advisories for extreme heat, flooding, winter storms and public safety incidents, and works with the Department of Public Health on health emergencies such as heat health emergencies and cooling centers. For large scheduled events, OEM coordinates security, transportation and crowd-management planning and publishes an annual report; its 2024 and 2025 end-of-year reports are available on phila.gov. [3]

3 Major events and responses

OEM's own history page lists, among major events, the prolonged activation of the Emergency Operations Center for the COVID-19 pandemic beginning in 2020, the response to flooding from Hurricane Ida in 2021 (for which OEM worked to obtain a major disaster declaration), and planning for large events including the 2015 papal visit and World Meeting of Families, the 2016 Democratic National Convention and the 2017 NFL Draft. [3] City-wide service disruptions such as the July 2025 municipal strike (see 2025 DC 33 Strike) are handled mainly by the affected departments and the Managing Director, with emergency-management tools available if needed. In 2026 the city described a coordinated multi-agency response to a historic summer of events, including the national 250th anniversary celebrations, FIFA World Cup matches and the MLB All-Star Game. [4] After-action reports are the usual means of recording lessons learned.

4 What residents can do

Residents can sign up for ReadyPhiladelphia alerts, make a household emergency plan, and check whether their address is in a flood-prone area. Renters should know how to reach their landlord during a disaster; tenant duties and protections are outlined in Rental Licensing and Landlord-Tenant Law in Philadelphia. Neighborhood groups can participate in community preparedness programs, and nonprofits can join the city's volunteer and community organizations active in disaster networks. Non-emergency questions and storm-damage reports can also be raised through Philly311; life-threatening emergencies always go to 911.

5 Relationship with state and federal agencies

When an emergency exceeds city capacity, OEM requests help from the Pennsylvania Emergency Management Agency (PEMA) and, through the Governor, from federal agencies such as FEMA. State and federal declarations determine eligibility for disaster aid; local declarations alone do not. The relationship is described in Pennsylvania State Government and Philadelphia and Federal Government and Philadelphia. The office is funded through the city budget and substantial federal homeland-security and emergency-management grants, which can fluctuate with federal appropriations.
