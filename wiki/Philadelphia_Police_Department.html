The Philadelphia Police Department (PPD) is the municipal law enforcement agency for the consolidated City and County of Philadelphia. It is one of the largest police departments in the United States and is a city department, not a county or state agency: its commissioner is appointed by the Mayor and the department operates within the executive branch described in the Home Rule Charter. The Pennsylvania State Police, the Sheriff's Office, university and transit police (such as SEPTA Transit Police) and federal agencies have separate jurisdictions that sometimes overlap with the PPD. [1]

1 Structure and leadership

The Police Commissioner is the department's chief executive and is a mayoral appointee. Kevin Bethel, a long-time member of the department, was named by Mayor Cherelle Parker and has led the department since her administration took office in January 2024. [2] The commissioner supervises deputy commissioners who run broad bureaus such as field operations, investigations, and organizational services. Within the administration the department answers to the Managing Director's Office, and Mayor Parker's administration also has an Office of Public Safety, which the city says is led by Chief Public Safety Director Adam Geer and which administers anti-violence programs and grants (see Office of Violence Prevention).

Patrol is organized into 21 police districts, grouped into divisions, each commanded by a captain or inspector. Specialized units include homicide, special victims, narcotics, the SWAT team, traffic, the marine and aviation units, and the Internal Affairs Division described in Police Accountability Systems. The department has used a Police Radio dispatch center for 911 police calls, while fire and medical dispatch are handled separately by the Fire Department.

2 Budget and staffing

Police is one of the largest line items in the city's General Fund, and most of its spending goes to personnel; see Philadelphia City Budget Process for how Council adopts the budget each spring and how to read the line items. NBC10 reported in January 2025 that the department had 5,021 sworn officers out of 6,500 authorized positions, a record low number of sworn officers despite increased recruiting. [3]

Recruits attend the Philadelphia Police Academy and serve a probationary period. Because most pay is set by collective bargaining and by interest arbitration awards (police and fire may not strike under Pennsylvania's Act 111), contract awards are a major driver of cost; see Public Sector Bargaining in Pennsylvania.

3 Working with residents

Residents reach the department by calling 911 for emergencies and the non-emergency line or Philly311 for other matters. Each district holds regular Police District Advisory Council (PDAC) meetings, which are open to the public and are a venue for neighborhood concerns. Residents can request incident reports through the department's records unit, and certain police records are accessible under the Right-to-Know Law subject to statutory exemptions for investigative material.

A person who wants to complain about an officer can file with the department's Internal Affairs Division or with the Citizens Police Oversight Commission; the process is outlined in How to File a Complaint. Criminal conduct by officers can also be reported to the District Attorney's Office or to federal authorities such as the U.S. Attorney's Office.

4 Discipline, oversight and the union

Officers are represented by Fraternal Order of Police Lodge 5. The collective bargaining agreement and arbitration decisions shape how discipline, body-camera policy and civilian oversight operate; the interaction is explained in The FOP Contract and Police Discipline. In 2025 interest arbitrators did not grant the oversight commission independent power to investigate officers, as reported by Axios; see Citizens Police Oversight Commission. [4]

5 History and public controversies

Frank Rizzo served as police commissioner from 1967 to 1971 and as mayor from 1972 to 1980. In 1979 the U.S. Department of Justice sued the city over alleged police abuse; the federal district court dismissed the suit in 1979 and the Third Circuit upheld the dismissal in United States v. City of Philadelphia, 644 F.2d 187, because the Justice Department lacked authority to bring it. [5]

On May 13, 1985, Philadelphia police aviators dropped two explosive devices from a Pennsylvania State Police helicopter onto the roof of the MOVE organization's row house on Osage Avenue. Six adults and five children died, and the ensuing fire, which the department allowed to burn for about 90 minutes, destroyed 61 neighboring houses. The city's Special Investigation Commission, in a report issued March 6, 1986, called dropping a bomb on an occupied row house unconscionable; Mayor W. Wilson Goode made a public apology afterward, and in November 2020 City Council approved a resolution formally apologizing for the bombing. [6]

In 2011 the city settled the Bailey v. City of Philadelphia stop-and-frisk class action (filed November 4, 2010) through a court-approved consent decree dated June 21, 2011, which requires the department to document stops and frisks in a database and to review them, with data reported to plaintiffs' counsel and the court; the ACLU of Pennsylvania has published periodic reports on the data. This is a decree from a private class action, not a federal consent decree arising from a Justice Department pattern-or-practice suit. In 2013, after an increase in officer-involved shootings, then-Commissioner Charles Ramsey asked the Justice Department's COPS Office for assistance; its March 2015 assessment of deadly-force practices made 48 findings and 91 recommendations on use-of-force policy and training. On June 1, 2020, police fired tear gas at protesters on Interstate 676; Mayor Jim Kenney and Commissioner Danielle Outlaw later apologized to the protesters. On June 9, 2026 the Justice Department announced a new investigation into whether the department's permit-revocation practices for firearm carry permits violate the Second Amendment. [7] In May 2025 a jury convicted former officer Mark Dial of voluntary manslaughter in the 2023 shooting death of Eddie Irizarry; he was acquitted of third-degree murder and official oppression, and on July 17, 2025 he was sentenced to nine and a half months to two years and granted immediate parole, a sentence the District Attorney publicly criticized. [8]
