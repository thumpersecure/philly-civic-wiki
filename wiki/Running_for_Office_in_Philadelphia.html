Running for Office in Philadelphia

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
Running for Office in Philadelphia
TypeCitizen guide
Filing officeCity Commissioners (nominating petitions for city offices)
Key rulesResidency, nominating petitions, resign-to-run, campaign finance limits and reporting
Governing lawPennsylvania Election Code; Philadelphia Home Rule Charter; Philadelphia Code Chapter 17-1400
OfficesMayor, Council (district and at-large), row offices, judges, party committee seats

Running for office in Philadelphia involves a mix of state election law, the city Charter and campaign finance rules enforced by the Board of Ethics. This article is a general orientation, not legal advice; prospective candidates should read the City Commissioners' candidate guide and consult the Board of Ethics before raising money.[1]

1 Choosing an office

City offices fill on a four-year cycle in odd-numbered years: mayor and 17 Council seats (2027 next), row offices such as City Controller and District Attorney (2025, next 2029), and judgeships; see Philadelphia Row Offices. State and federal offices run in even-numbered years. The lowest-barrier entry point is party committeeperson; see Ward Leaders and Committeepersons.

2 Qualifications and petitions

The Charter sets residency requirements for mayoral and Council candidates (and district Council candidates must live in their district); the exact lengths are in the Charter and should be confirmed with the Commissioners before circulating petitions. To appear on a primary ballot, a candidate files nominating petitions with a required number of valid signatures from registered voters of the candidate's party; the required number varies by office and is set by the Pennsylvania Election Code, so candidates should get the exact figure for their office and cycle from the City Commissioners. Petition circulation begins in February and petitions are due in early March of the election year, according to the City Commissioners' calendar for recent cycles. Petitions are routinely challenged in court on technical grounds such as invalid signatures, so candidates usually collect well beyond the minimum.[2] Candidates also file a statement of financial interests with the Board of Ethics.

3 The resign-to-run rule

Under Article X of the Home Rule Charter, city officers and employees must resign before running for another public office, with limited exceptions. The rule has led several officials to resign their posts to seek other offices, notably in advance of the 2023 mayor's race. Candidates running for their current office do not need to resign. Reformers and some officials have debated the rule; see Ballot Questions and Charter Changes.

4 Forming a campaign

A candidate must register a campaign committee, open a bank account, and file periodic finance reports. Contribution limits apply per source; see Campaign Finance in Philadelphia. Public employees' political activity is restricted by the Charter, the civil service rules and ethics law; see Conflicts of Interest and Pay-to-Play.

5 Party endorsement and the primary

In a city where Democrats dominate, the Democratic primary is usually decisive. The party committee and ward leaders endorse before the primary, and labor unions and groups like Reclaim Philadelphia or the Working Families Party also endorse; see Democratic City Committee and Progressive and Grassroots Politics. Minor-party and independent candidates qualify through nomination papers rather than a primary. After the primary, the general election in November follows; see Elections in Philadelphia.
