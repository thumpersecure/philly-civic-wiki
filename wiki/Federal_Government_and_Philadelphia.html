Federal Government and Philadelphia

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
Federal government and Philadelphia

The federal government affects Philadelphia through money, courts, law enforcement and regulation. Federal law is supreme over state and local law, and federal funding supports large parts of the city budget, but the city retains independent authority to decide how to use its own officers and resources. The relationship has been particularly contested over immigration enforcement.[1]

1 Funding

Federal dollars reach Philadelphia through block grants such as Community Development Block Grants, housing vouchers and public housing support, transit capital funds, Medicaid and public-health grants, education aid including Title I, and disaster relief. Much of this flows through state agencies or directly to city departments and authorities such as the Philadelphia Housing Authority, SEPTA and the School District. Because of that dependence, city budget planners treat federal policy shifts as a risk: for fiscal 2026, Mayor Cherelle Parker proposed a $95 million reserve in anticipation of possible federal funding cuts.[2] See Philadelphia City Budget Process.

2 Federal courts and prosecutors

Philadelphia sits in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania, whose federal trial court hears civil rights, immigration-related, bankruptcy and criminal cases, with appeals to the Third Circuit in Philadelphia. The U.S. Attorney's Office prosecutes federal crimes and has brought public corruption cases against city and union officials (see Political Corruption Cases in Philadelphia). Federal courts also review local policing and jail conditions, as in long-running consent decrees and class actions affecting the Department of Prisons. Local prosecution is separate: the District Attorney handles state-law crimes.

3 Immigration and "sanctuary" policy

Philadelphia's policies limit city cooperation with federal civil immigration enforcement, and the city describes itself as a welcoming city (see Office of Immigrant Affairs). During President Trump's first term, the Justice Department sought to condition certain grants on cooperation; a federal judge sided with Philadelphia in 2018. In August 2025 the Justice Department published a list of 35 jurisdictions it described as sanctuary jurisdictions, according to CBS News. In June 2026 the Trump administration sued Philadelphia over its "ICE Out" law, according to the South Philly Review. On July 2, 2026, a federal judge ruled that Philadelphia could not enforce its ban on masking against federal immigration agents, as reported by WHYY and the Inquirer, and in August 2026 the Inquirer reported that the Justice Department was targeting another "ICE Out" ordinance.[3] As of October 2026 the litigation and related funding conditions were still developing; readers should consult current coverage. The legal arguments typically invoke the Tenth Amendment, which limits federal commandeering of local resources, against the federal government's authority over immigration.

4 Housing, transit and other agencies

Federal agencies such as HUD, the Department of Transportation, the Environmental Protection Agency and the Department of Education impose requirements on city programs as a condition of money. Federal civil rights law, the Fair Housing Act, and the Americans with Disabilities Act also apply to local government. Federal elections are administered locally by the City Commissioners under state and federal law. Members of Congress representing Philadelphia help the city advocate in Washington (see Philadelphia Congressional and State Delegation). Federal rules sit above, and sometimes override, both state and local law (see Home Rule Limits and State Preemption).
