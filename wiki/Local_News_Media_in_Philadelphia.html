Local News Media in Philadelphia

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
Local News Media in Philadelphia
Largest newsroomThe Philadelphia Inquirer (owned by the nonprofit Lenfest Institute for Journalism through a public benefit corporation)
Other outletsPhiladelphia Tribune; WHYY and Billy Penn; The Philadelphia Citizen; Spotlight PA (statewide); Axios Philadelphia; neighborhood and ethnic-community papers; broadcast TV and radio
Ownership modelMix of nonprofit, public-media, family-owned and commercial
Legal toolsPennsylvania Right-to-Know Law; Sunshine Act

Local news media in Philadelphia are the newspapers, public and commercial broadcasters and digital outlets that report on city government, schools, courts and neighborhoods. Their coverage is a primary way residents learn about the work of the City Council, the Police Department and other bodies, and their investigative reporting has driven several of the reform episodes in Political Corruption Cases in Philadelphia. [1]

1 The Inquirer and the Lenfest Institute

The Philadelphia Inquirer is a public benefit corporation owned by the nonprofit Lenfest Institute for Journalism. Businessman H.F. "Gerry" Lenfest bought Philadelphia Media Network, the Inquirer's parent company, in June 2014 and in 2016 donated it to The Philadelphia Foundation so that the papers would remain in Philadelphia; the Lenfest Institute for Journalism, a nonprofit, holds the newsroom, a structure widely described as the first of its kind in which a nonprofit owns a for-profit newsroom. The Philadelphia Daily News, a tabloid founded in 1925, became an edition of the Inquirer in 2019. The Institute reports having invested more than $117 million in over 600 news organizations and initiatives. The Inquirer reported its first revenue growth since 2004 in 2025, along with an operating profit of several million dollars, according to coverage of its finances. Nonprofit ownership means that the paper is insulated from a single owner's commercial or political interests but still depends on subscribers, advertisers and philanthropy (see Philanthropy in Philadelphia). [2]

2 The Philadelphia Tribune

The Philadelphia Tribune, founded in 1884, is regarded as the nation's oldest continuously published Black newspaper. It covers political developments of particular interest to Black Philadelphians, including clergy, ward and school-board politics (see Faith Communities and Black Clergy), and is regularly cited in reporting on local candidates. Other community papers serve the Northeast, South Philadelphia, Spanish-speaking, Asian American and other communities.

3 Public media and digital outlets

WHYY, the region's public broadcaster, operates radio, television and its news site; Billy Penn, a local digital outlet, joined WHYY. WHYY has had staff reductions in past years. The Philadelphia Citizen is a nonprofit news and civic-engagement outlet. Spotlight PA is a statewide nonprofit newsroom covering Harrisburg, which matters for a city whose finances and powers depend on state law (see Home Rule Limits and State Preemption). Chalkbeat Philadelphia covers education, and national newsletters such as Axios have local editions. Television and radio stations provide breaking and election coverage. [3]

4 Economics and consolidation

Like local news elsewhere, Philadelphia outlets have faced declining print advertising, layoffs and mergers over two decades. Examples include the merger of the Inquirer, Daily News and Philly.com newsrooms with tens of layoffs, the end of City Paper's print run and WHYY layoffs of about 8 percent of staff in a prior restructuring. New nonprofit and digital outlets have partly offset the loss, but coverage of some neighborhoods and of routine government meetings has thinned, a pattern researchers call a news gap.

5 Media as a political actor

Editorial endorsements, investigative series and agenda-setting coverage influence campaigns and policy debates, and politicians use media and social media to speak around reporters. Debates include whether coverage focuses on conflict over policy substance, how outlets disclose ties to funders, and whether the City provides records and press access promptly. Journalists and civic groups have pressed for stronger open-records enforcement; see Right-to-Know Law and Public Records.

6 Using records laws

Journalists and residents use the Right-to-Know Law and the Sunshine Act. For a how-to, see How to Request Public Records.
