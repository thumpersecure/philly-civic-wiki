Safe and Healthy Homes Act

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
Safe and Healthy Homes Act
TypePackage of two City Council bills amending the Philadelphia Code
BillsBill No. 250329 ("Right to Repairs"); Bill No. 250330 ("Right to Safety")
IntroducedSpring 2025
Final Council voteApril 23, 2026 (passed 16-1, after a court settlement sent the bills back to committee)
MayorCherelle Parker; signing reported May 7, 2026
Effective dateNovember 1, 2026
EnforcementLicenses and Inspections, Fair Housing Commission, courts

The Safe and Healthy Homes Act is the name used for two Philadelphia City Council bills, 250329 and 250330, that change the rules for rental housing in the city. Supporters, including tenant advocates, framed them as strengthening repair, safety and anti-retaliation protections; some landlords challenged the process and argued the measures did not target bad actors. This article summarizes publicly reported provisions and is not legal advice. Readers should confirm current requirements and forms with the City.

1 Background and legislative history

The bills were introduced in City Council in spring 2025 and moved through the Housing Committee over the following year. They build on existing law: Philadelphia already requires rental licenses and Certificates of Rental Suitability and bars certain unfair rental practices (see Rental Licensing and Landlord-Tenant Law in Philadelphia). Advocates argued that enforcement gaps allowed owners with serious unresolved violations to avoid accountability and that tenants who complained risked retaliation. A Council housing committee action in 2026 also backed a budget request for implementation, reflecting that inspections and enforcement would require resources (see Philadelphia City Budget Process).[1]

2 Bill 250329: Right to Repairs

Bill 250329 updates the rental license and Certificate of Rental Suitability provisions so that landlords with serious, unaddressed code violations cannot evade detection and accountability.[1] According to summaries by a public-interest law group and a law-firm client alert, key provisions include: landlords must correct any unsafe, unfit or imminently dangerous violations within 30 days of notice unless a timely appeal is filed, and failure to do so makes the landlord noncompliant; landlords may not collect rent while out of compliance or during license suspension; tenants are entitled to rent abatement or a refund for periods in which rent was collected without a valid rental license; evictions are barred where the landlord has outstanding violations; and licensing and violation information must be posted and made transparent. The law also authorizes the City to create a proactive rental inspection program, rather than inspecting only after complaints.[2]

3 Bill 250330: Right to Safety

Bill 250330 strengthens anti-retaliation protections by broadening what counts as retaliation, adds protections for tenants who organize, and establishes new protections related to habitability and harassment. It also expands good-cause protections so they apply to all tenants regardless of lease type, and requires landlords to provide written notices to renters regarding lease terminations.[1] Tenants may seek remedies in court, including damages reported as $1,000 per violation or actual compensatory damages, whichever is greater, plus attorney's fees.[2]

4 Lawsuit and passage

In March 2026, two landlords sued, alleging that Council violated Pennsylvania's Sunshine Act because public comment was opened only after the bills passed through the Housing Committee. The suit delayed a scheduled Council vote (see Sunshine Act and Open Meetings).[3] The Inquirer and WHYY reported that the case was settled on March 18, 2026, the day before the scheduled vote, with the bills returning to the Housing Committee for a new hearing; the committee voted again on March 30.[4] The plaintiffs, reported as property owners Erica Hadley and Seth Floyd, then sought to hold the city in contempt and added constitutional claims; a second challenge was reported in federal court, where a judge was reported to have denied most of the requests, and Council gave final approval on April 23, 2026, by a 16-1 vote.[5] The Inquirer reported on May 7, 2026, that Mayor Cherelle Parker had signed the bills and that a contempt hearing was scheduled for June 2026; the outcome of that hearing and the later status of the litigation should be checked in court records and current news reports.[5]

5 Effective date and implementation

The legislation takes effect on November 1, 2026, according to the Inquirer, Bisnow and a Legal Intelligencer analysis.[5][6] Implementation depends on L&I and other agencies issuing forms, notices and procedures, funding for any proactive inspection program, and training for the Fair Housing Commission and courts. Landlords are advised by counsel and trade groups to review licensing, certificates, violation histories and lease notice practices ahead of the effective date (see Landlord and Property Owner Associations). Tenants can find guidance from advocacy groups and legal aid (see Tenant and Housing Advocacy Groups).

6 Positions and open questions

Supporters say the package fills gaps that let unsafe conditions persist and discourages retaliation. Landlord organizations and some owners have said the measures add compliance risk and liability, and the plaintiffs in the 2026 suit said the legislation did not hold bad actors accountable.[4] Open questions include the pace of inspections, how the new good-cause rules interact with state law governing evictions (see Home Rule Limits and State Preemption), and how courts will treat the new private right of action. See Eviction Diversion and Landlord-Tenant Court for the court process.
