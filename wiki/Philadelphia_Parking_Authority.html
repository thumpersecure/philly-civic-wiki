Philadelphia Parking Authority

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works

The Philadelphia Parking Authority (PPA) is a public authority that operates and regulates much of the city's parking system. Created by a city ordinance in 1950 and governed under state law, it is separate from the city government, though it generates substantial revenue for the City and the School District of Philadelphia. Residents most often encounter it as the issuer of parking tickets, the operator of garages and lots, and, historically, the regulator of taxis and limousines.[1]

1 Structure and governance

The PPA was created by a City Ordinance enacted on January 11, 1950, but state law has since moved control to Harrisburg: the PPA describes itself as governed by a six-member board appointed by the Governor of Pennsylvania under Act 22 of 2001, and it is led by an executive director chosen by the board.[1] This reflects the state's historical role in the authority, which is why proposals to change its structure usually involve the General Assembly (see Pennsylvania State Government and Philadelphia and Home Rule Limits and State Preemption). The City Council and mayor do not control its day-to-day operations.

2 Functions

  • On-street parking. The PPA manages meters and kiosks, residential permit parking enforcement and ticketing, and towing or booting for unpaid tickets.
  • Off-street parking. It operates garages and lots across the city, including facilities in Center City and neighborhoods. At Philadelphia International Airport the PPA has operated the parking facilities since the mid-1970s; in January 2022 the City's Department of Aviation paid off more than $54 million of PPA bonds tied to the airport garages, giving the airport greater control over the garage complex (see Philadelphia International Airport).[2]
  • Taxi and limousine regulation. Under state law the PPA regulates taxis and limousines in Philadelphia, including medallion and driver rules, and enforces rules relating to ride-hailing services.
  • Automated enforcement. It has been given responsibilities for some camera-based enforcement under state law, such as bus-lane and certain speed-camera programs, though the legal framework is set in state statute.
  • Adjudication. Parking ticket disputes are heard by the PPA's Bureau of Administrative Adjudication (sometimes called parking court), with appeals going to the Court of Common Pleas (see Court of Common Pleas and Municipal Court).

3 Money and revenue sharing

Revenue comes from parking fees and fines, garage operations, regulatory fees, and debt collection. After operating costs, the PPA makes payments to the City. Act 9 of 2004 created a formula under which, once the PPA reaches a $25 million threshold for its payment to the City, additional on-street parking revenue can go to the School District. According to the PPA, since 2004 it has contributed $577 million to the City and $132 million to the School District.[3] The formula has drawn scrutiny; a 2022 City Council resolution sought a review of the figures the PPA used in determining the school payment. Activists have also argued for more ticket-derived revenue for schools. These payments are part of the funding picture described in Philadelphia City Budget Process.

4 Leadership changes and audits

Richard Lazer, a former deputy mayor for labor, became executive director in 2022 and left effective May 31, 2026, to become executive director and CEO of PhilaPort (see Port and Bridge Authorities). Deputy Director Gabe Roberts, who joined the PPA in 2023 after twelve years with the Philadelphia courts, became interim executive director on June 1, 2026.[4] The PPA's website was advertising the executive director position in October 2026, and no permanent successor had been announced as of that date. Earlier, in 2018, the City Controller and the state Auditor General announced a joint performance audit of the authority, and a report was issued in December 2020.[5] The PPA has also been the subject of past leadership controversies and legislative proposals to restructure it; readers should consult specific reports and news coverage for details.

5 Tickets, appeals and complaints

A person who receives a ticket generally can pay it, request a hearing, or appeal to the Court of Common Pleas after an adverse decision. Information on payment, hearings and permits is on the PPA's website. Complaints about customer service can be raised with the PPA and, for city-related matters, through 311; oversight questions can be raised with the City Controller or state legislators. The PPA is also covered by the Right-to-Know Law and the Sunshine Act.

6 Debates

Common topics include whether the PPA should remain a state-created authority, how its revenue is divided between the City, schools and its own operations, how enforcement affects neighborhoods and small businesses, and how curb space should be managed. Transportation planners connect parking policy to the city's broader street policies (see Office of Transportation, Infrastructure and Sustainability and Streets Department). The PPA's defenders note its role in producing steady revenue for public purposes.
