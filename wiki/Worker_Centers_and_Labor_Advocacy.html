Worker Centers and Labor Advocacy

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
Worker Centers and Labor Advocacy
TypeCommunity-based nonprofit organizations and coalitions
ExamplesJuntos; Philadelphia Workers Association; domestic worker organizing; Coalition of Labor Union Women chapter
ServesLow-wage, immigrant, domestic and gig workers; workers without union contracts
Related city officeOffice of Worker Protections

Worker centers are community-based organizations that provide education, legal referrals, organizing support and advocacy for workers who generally lack a union contract. Unlike unions, they typically do not bargain collective agreements; they instead pursue wage claims, policy changes and public campaigns. In Philadelphia they operate alongside unions and the Office of Worker Protections.[1]

1 What worker centers do

Typical activities include know-your-rights workshops, assistance filing wage theft and retaliation complaints, English classes, leadership training and organizing campaigns against specific employers. Because many members are immigrants or work informally, centers often coordinate with legal aid providers; see Legal Aid and Public Interest Law. Law school clinics have also supported the model; Temple University's Center for Social Justice has published guidance on creating a worker center in Philadelphia.[2]

2 Organizations in Philadelphia

Juntos is a community-led Latinx immigrant organization in South Philadelphia that combines leadership development with organizing around workers' rights, education and immigration. The Philadelphia Workers Association is another group listed in nonprofit directories. National domestic worker organizations took part in public hearings on the 2025 POWER Act. The Philadelphia chapter of the Coalition of Labor Union Women, led by Koren Parker according to City & State's 2026 list, connects union women and community advocates. Unions such as UNITE HERE Local 274 and 32BJ SEIU also run community organizing programs that overlap with worker centers. The AFL-CIO council frequently partners with them.

3 Policy campaigns

Worker advocates were prominent in supporting the POWER Act and earlier measures such as paid sick leave and Fair Workweek, and in pressing for enforcement resources in the city budget. They also engage with immigration policy; see Office of Immigrant Affairs. Some centers receive foundation support; see Philanthropy in Philadelphia. Their political role is discussed in Labor and Politics in Philadelphia and Progressive and Grassroots Politics.

4 Getting help

Workers with a pay or retaliation problem can contact the city's Office of Worker Protections, the state Department of Labor and Industry or the federal Wage and Hour Division, or a worker center or legal aid group. Tenants who are also workers may find overlapping help in Tenant and Housing Advocacy Groups. This is general information and not legal advice.
