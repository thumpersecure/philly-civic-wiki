Philadelphia Historical Commission

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
Philadelphia Historical Commission
TypeCity commission (Home Rule Charter and Philadelphia Code Title 14)

Address1515 Arch Street, 13th Floor; (215) 686-7660

The Philadelphia Historical Commission protects the city's historic resources by designating landmarks and districts, maintaining the Philadelphia Register of Historic Places, and reviewing building permit applications for work on listed properties.[1] It is a city commission, separate from the state Pennsylvania Historical and Museum Commission and the federal National Register of Historic Places, although a property can be on more than one list. Only the local register carries legal review of exterior changes and demolition.

1 Structure and committees

The Commission consists of nine members appointed by the Mayor, including a real estate developer who serves as chair, an architect, a historian, an architectural historian and representatives of community organizations and development corporations, plus five ex officio members: the City Council President, the City Planning Commission chair, and directors or commissioners from Commerce, Planning and Development, Licenses and Inspections, and Public Property (one appointed seat was vacant on the City's page in October 2026). It is staffed by preservation planners within the Department of Planning and Development and is led by an executive director.[1] It meets monthly, in person and by Zoom, and does much of its detailed work through advisory committees: the Committee on Historic Designation reviews nominations, and the Architectural Committee reviews permit proposals. Agendas, applications and minutes are posted publicly.

2 Designation process

  1. Nomination. Anyone, including residents, civic groups, owners or the Commission itself, can nominate a building, object, site or district, using a nomination form that documents its significance against criteria in the Code.
  2. Notice. The owner and the relevant City Council office are notified before review.
  3. Committee on Historic Designation holds a public meeting and makes a recommendation.
  4. Commission vote. The full Commission votes at its monthly meeting. Listing places the property on the Register.

Designations can be rescinded; for example, the Committee on Historic Designation agenda of July 22, 2026 included a rescission matter for a property on West Montgomery Avenue.[2]

3 Permit review

When an owner of a listed property applies to L&I for a permit affecting the exterior (or the interior of designated public spaces), the application is routed to the Commission. According to the city, staff approve over 90 percent of building permit applications without referral to a committee.[1] The rest go to the Architectural Committee and then, if needed, to the Commission. Standards follow the Secretary of the Interior's Standards for Rehabilitation as adopted in Commission rules.

4 Demolition, hardship and appeals

Demolition of a listed building requires Commission approval. An owner may seek relief by showing that the building cannot be used for a reasonable purpose or that demolition is necessary in the public interest, commonly called a financial-hardship or public-interest claim; such applications are decided by the Commission after a hearing. The Commission's decisions can be appealed, and the city identifies the Board of License and Inspection Review as one body that hears some appeals involving the Commission.[3] Because the correct forum and deadlines depend on the type of decision, applicants should confirm on phila.gov and may wish to consult a lawyer. This article is general information, not legal advice.

5 Controversy and public participation

Preservation sits in tension with housing production and property rights. Preservation groups argue designation protects neighborhood character and heritage; some owners and developers argue it limits use of their property and raises costs. The Commission's approach, including how aggressively to designate, has been the subject of City Council and press debate over many years. Residents may nominate properties, attend meetings and submit written comment before votes. Related design review for public projects is done by the Art Commission, also within the planning cluster, and zoning relief for the same sites goes separately to the Zoning Board of Adjustment.
