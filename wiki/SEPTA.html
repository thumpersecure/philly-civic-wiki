The Southeastern Pennsylvania Transportation Authority (SEPTA) is the regional transit agency that runs most public transportation in Philadelphia and its four surrounding counties. It is a state-created authority, not a city department, and it depends on funding from the Commonwealth, the federal government, riders and local governments. Its board and finances are therefore shaped as much by Harrisburg as by Philadelphia City Hall.[1]

1 Governance

SEPTA is governed by a 15-member board of directors. According to public summaries of its enabling law, the City of Philadelphia appoints two members (one by the mayor and one by the Council President); Bucks, Chester, Delaware and Montgomery counties appoint two each, through county commissioners or, in Delaware County, county council; the majority and minority leaders of the state Senate and House each appoint one; and the governor appoints one. The two Philadelphia members can veto board actions because the city accounts for the majority of SEPTA's local subsidy, fares and ridership, but a veto can be overridden by a vote of at least 75% of the full board within 30 days.[2] The agency is run by a general manager and chief executive, Scott Sauer, who received a three-year contract in February 2026, according to the Inquirer.[7] Its board meetings are public (see Sunshine Act and Open Meetings).

2 Services

SEPTA operates city and suburban bus routes, the Market-Frankford and Broad Street subway lines (the Broad Street line includes a spur), trolley lines, trackless trolleys and Regional Rail commuter lines connecting Center City with surrounding communities. It also runs paratransit service and a transit police department. In public summaries SEPTA is described as serving about 3.9 million people in five counties.[2] Planning for streets, bus lanes and bike facilities involves the City as well (see Office of Transportation, Infrastructure and Sustainability and Streets Department).

3 Funding

SEPTA's operating budget is financed by passenger fares, state operating assistance, local contributions and other revenue, while major capital projects rely heavily on federal and state grants. Pennsylvania's transit funding framework has depended on state laws that dedicate money to transit; long-term proposals to add recurring state funding have been debated repeatedly in Harrisburg (see Pennsylvania State Government and Philadelphia). Because operating funds and capital funds are separate legal pots, shifting money between them requires state permission.

4 The 2025 budget crisis

In June 2025 SEPTA's board approved a fiscal year 2026 budget that, absent new state funding, would have cut service by roughly 45% in stages, facing a deficit of about $213 million. The plan included a 20% service cut and 21.5% fare increase beginning in the late summer of 2025 and further cuts in January 2026, including the elimination of five Regional Rail lines.[3] A Philadelphia judge's order required restoration of service, and in September 2025 PennDOT approved a one-time flex of $394 million in state capital assistance to cover operating costs; SEPTA restored service on September 14, 2025, while fare increases proceeded.[4] The state budget adopted later in 2025 did not include new recurring transit funding, and SEPTA's CEO called that "disappointing." In late October 2025 the board voted to shift capital funds to operations to avoid further cuts, and reporting indicates the stopgap funding runs to about mid-2027, with advocates and officials warning of a "funding cliff" after a strong summer of 2026 ridership.[5] SEPTA's board approved its fiscal year 2027 operating and capital budgets in June 2026, and the $50.8 billion Pennsylvania budget signed in July 2026 was reported not to include new recurring state transit funding for Pittsburgh Regional Transit, a sign that the funding debate was not settled; the long-term solution remained unresolved as of October 2026.[8]

5 Labor and workforce

SEPTA's largest bargaining unit, Transport Workers Union Local 234, reached a tentative two-year agreement with SEPTA on December 8, 2025, averting a strike; members ratified it on December 17, 2025 and the SEPTA board approved it the next day, according to CBS News, NBC10 and the Inquirer[6] (see TWU Local 234 and SEPTA Labor). In June 2026 WHYY reported a tentative agreement between SEPTA and its transit police, reached to avoid a strike during the World Cup. Public-sector labor relations for transit are governed by state law (see Public Sector Bargaining in Pennsylvania).

6 Riders, complaints and civic role

Riders can file complaints and service requests with SEPTA's customer service and attend board meetings or the Citizens Advisory Committee. Philadelphia officials, advocacy groups and business groups, including the Chamber of Commerce for Greater Philadelphia, have all lobbied for stable funding. Residents wishing to influence decisions can contact their elected officials, including state legislators, because the state sets funding policy.
