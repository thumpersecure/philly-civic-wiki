City-County Consolidation

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
City-County Consolidation
TypeState statute merging a city with its surrounding county
EnactedFebruary 2, 1854 (Pennsylvania General Assembly)
EffectCity boundaries expanded to match Philadelphia County
Later changeCounty offices folded into city government under the 1951 Home Rule Charter and related constitutional amendments
Surviving legacyElected "row offices"; Philadelphia is one of the few places where city and county are identical

City-County Consolidation refers to the 1854 act of the Pennsylvania legislature that merged the old City of Philadelphia, then a small area between the Delaware and Schuylkill rivers, with every other municipality in Philadelphia County. The result was a single city whose boundaries equal those of the county. It remains the defining fact of local government structure: there is no separate county government, and the city performs county functions.[1]

1 Before 1854

In the early nineteenth century the chartered city was only a few square miles. Around it in Philadelphia County sat independent districts, boroughs and townships, including Northern Liberties, Spring Garden, Southwark, Moyamensing, Kensington, and West Philadelphia, each with its own officials, police and services. Industrialization and population growth made the patchwork difficult to govern. Fire companies and gangs crossed jurisdictional lines, disorder was a recurrent problem (including the May 1844 anti-Catholic riots in Kensington), and reformers argued that a unified government would deliver better policing, water and infrastructure. These pressures drove the push for merger.[2]

2 The Act of Consolidation

The General Assembly passed the consolidation act, signed by Governor William Bigler on February 2, 1854. It extended the city's boundaries to the county line, abolished the separate municipal governments, and gave the enlarged city a single mayor and council structure with authority over services across the whole territory. The act absorbed the remaining townships, boroughs and districts of the county into the city. It was a state law rather than a local choice, a reminder that Pennsylvania's cities derive their powers from the commonwealth.

3 From two governments to one

For nearly a century after 1854 the city and county remained legally distinct entities, even though they covered the same territory. Each had its own officers: city government handled municipal services while the county had its own elected officials such as the sheriff, register of wills, and city commissioners. Reform advocates considered the duplication inefficient and a source of patronage. In 1951 Pennsylvania voters amended the state constitution to allow cities and counties to fully merge, and Philadelphia's Home Rule Charter, which took effect in January 1952, merged nearly all city and county institutions into one government, although some county row offices persisted as independently elected offices.

4 What survives today

Several former county offices remain as independently elected row offices: the City Controller, the District Attorney, the Sheriff, the City Commissioners and the Register of Wills and Clerk of Orphans' Court. The county courts of Philadelphia are state courts within the First Judicial District. Philadelphia's county-level functions, such as property assessment, elections and the courts' administration, are therefore split among city departments, row offices, and the state judiciary, a division that confuses many residents.

5 Why it matters

Consolidation means that city taxes fund services that elsewhere in Pennsylvania are split between municipalities and a county, and that a single set of voters elects both city and county officials. It also means that Philadelphia's mayor is the closest equivalent to both a mayor and a county executive. Comparisons with other consolidations, such as those in Denver, Nashville and Louisville, are common in academic literature.
