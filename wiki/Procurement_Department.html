Procurement Department

The Procurement Department is the City of Philadelphia's central purchasing agency. Its stated mission is "managing and acquiring goods and services for the City of Philadelphia using a fair, open, and cost-effective process."[1] It is the office that issues bids and requests for proposals, certifies vendors' compliance with City rules and awards contracts for supplies, equipment, services, concessions and public works, alongside departments that run their own program-specific procurements. It sits within the cluster overseen by the Chief Administrative Officer. Mayor Cherelle Parker named Ronald L. Hovey as Procurement Commissioner in March 2025; his background includes nearly 40 years in public and private sectors, including Amtrak and New Jersey Transit, and negotiation of the contracts for the $1.5 billion Portal North Bridge replacement.[2]

1 How City purchasing works

Contracts valued over $34,000 require formal bidding through the PHLContracts platform. Businesses find opportunities on the Contracts Hub at contracts.phila.gov and submit bids electronically following the instructions in each solicitation.[1] Typical opportunities include supplies, equipment maintenance and repair, concessions on City property and environmentally responsible waste disposal. The department also supports vendors through a "PVCOOP" assistance contact. Smaller purchases may follow simpler quotation procedures. Contracts above certain amounts need approval steps involving the Law Department for form and legality, and the Finance Department for funds and risk management. For a step-by-step guide see Understanding City Contracts and Bids.

2 Public works and professional services

Construction or improvement of public property, streets, bridges, highways and sewers follows a separate public-works bidding process.[1] Professional-services contracts, such as legal, consulting and investment management work, are generally selected through requests for proposals reviewed by committee. Departments report the dollar amounts awarded and the participation of minority-, woman- and disabled-owned firms in annual Council budget testimony; for example, the Finance Department reported $28 million in professional-services contracts for FY26 with 30 percent going to M/W/DSBE firms.[3] Conflict rules for firms that give to campaigns are summarized in Conflicts of Interest and Pay-to-Play.

3 Inclusion and small-business goals

The City sets a goal that 35 percent of contract dollars go to minority, woman and disabled-owned businesses (M/W/DSBEs) each year, monitored by the Office of Economic Opportunity.[4] The administration has also been building a registry of small and local businesses (S/LBEs) and setting participation goals for them; department testimony in 2026 lists goals in the range of 25 to 40 percent depending on the department, with some departments noting that S/LBE data are not yet available for earlier years.[3] In March 2025 Mayor Parker also named Nadir Jones as Director of Business Impact and Supplier Diversity in an Office of Business Impact and Economic Advancement, an office listed in the same announcement as Hovey's appointment.[2]

4 Systems modernization

Procurement is a partner in Project OPAL, the replacement of the City's accounting, contracting and procurement systems led by Finance with the CAO and the Office of Innovation and Technology, targeted to go live in July 2027.[3] The CAO's 2026 testimony said the administration streamlined the professional-services contract conformance process to give vendors fewer hurdles and clearer timelines and templates, and Procurement supported several citywide events in 2026.[5] Procurement's FY27 General Fund appropriation was about $7.9 million.[6] A purchasing-card (P-Card) pilot run with Finance and the City Treasurer aims to speed small purchases.

5 Oversight and ethics

City purchasing is subject to Charter and Philadelphia Code requirements, review by the City Controller, who must audit and can examine contracts and payments, and investigation by the Office of the Inspector General. Procurement officials and bidders are also covered by the rules of the Board of Ethics. Residents may report suspected fraud or waste on a contract to the Office of Economic Opportunity or the Inspector General; see How to Report Corruption or Misconduct.
