Parks and Recreation

Philadelphia Parks & Recreation (PPR) is the city department that operates and maintains the city's park system, recreation centers, playgrounds, pools, golf and trail facilities, and street and park trees, and that runs recreation, aquatics, and sports programs. It is led by a commissioner appointed by the mayor; Susan Slawson was appointed to the position in 2024 under Mayor Cherelle Parker.[1]

1 History and structure

Fairmount Park, one of the nation's largest urban park systems, was long governed by an independent Fairmount Park Commission. After a Charter change, the commission and the former Recreation Department were merged into a single department; Parks & Recreation became their successor in 2010 under the mayor's cabinet.[2] The department's work is coordinated within the Managing Director's cabinet (see Managing Director's Office) and sits alongside the Mayor's Clean and Green initiatives. Its employees are largely represented by District Council 33 and, for certain trades, other unions.

2 Facilities and programs

PPR operates a citywide network of recreation centers, playgrounds and playing fields, outdoor and indoor pools, ice rinks and cultural sites. Programs include after-school and summer camps, aquatics, senior fitness, athletics and arts. It also includes Philadelphia's urban forestry work and the planting of street trees, working with the Office of Sustainability on climate resilience and with the Water Department on green stormwater infrastructure.

The City describes the department as managing more than 10,200 acres of public land and waterways.[4] For information, residents can call (215) 683-3600 or write to parksandrecreation@phila.gov. Philadelphia City Council's announcement of the FY27 budget in June 2026 listed workforce, violence-prevention and library investments, and advocates have argued over how parks and recreation centers fare in the same cycle.

3 Permits and public use

Residents can apply to PPR for permits to use fields, pavilions and other park spaces and to hold events, and can register for programs at recreation centers. Large events on parkland, such as festivals and races, require permits and coordination with other city agencies. Concerns about maintenance, safety or hazards can be reported through Philly311, or by contacting the local recreation center or the district Council member.

4 Funding, Rebuild and partners

PPR is funded through the city budget, grants and private philanthropy. The city's beverage tax, enacted in 2016, has funded Rebuild, a capital program to renovate parks, recreation centers and libraries, alongside universal pre-K; see Philadelphia Taxes. Nonprofit partners include "friends of" groups and the Philadelphia Parks & Recreation Conservancy, on whose board the commissioner holds an ex officio seat.[1] Council members and advocates have urged larger budgets for parks and libraries in recent budget cycles.[3]

5 Debates

Recurring issues include deferred maintenance, equity in investment across neighborhoods, staffing for recreation centers and pools, and the balance between public funding and philanthropy. Reports and hearings in Council examine these questions annually, and the department's relationship with the Free Library in sharing Rebuild capital funds is a frequent topic.
