Charter Schools in Philadelphia

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
Charter Schools in Philadelphia
TypePublicly funded schools run by independent nonprofit boards
Enrollment64,469 students in October 2025 (about 32.5 percent of 198,405 public-school students)
AuthorizerPhiladelphia Board of Education (brick-and-mortar charters); Pennsylvania Department of Education (cyber charters)
Governing lawPennsylvania Charter School Law (Act 22 of 1997, as amended)
AppealsState Charter Appeal Board; Commonwealth Court

Charter schools in Philadelphia are public schools that operate under a charter granted by a public authorizer but are managed by independent nonprofit boards rather than by the School District of Philadelphia. Philadelphia has one of the highest charter shares of any large American city: the District reported 64,469 charter students on October 1, 2025, about one third of all public-school students in the city. [1]

1 Legal framework

Charters in Pennsylvania are governed by the 1997 Charter School Law, a state statute. A local school board (here, the Board of Education) can grant, renew, amend or revoke a brick-and-mortar charter, typically for five-year terms. Applicants and charter operators can appeal adverse decisions to the State Charter Appeal Board and then to the courts. Cyber charter schools are chartered by the state Department of Education, not by the District, even though the District pays tuition for Philadelphia students who enroll in them. Charter schools are subject to the Right-to-Know Law and the Sunshine Act, though disputes about their transparency have arisen. [2]

2 History in Philadelphia

Charter growth accelerated during the era of the School Reform Commission (2001-2018), when the state-influenced body authorized new charters and invited private managers to run some District schools. Enrollment in charters then plateaued: the District's data show a 1.6 percent rise, or 1,028 students, in charter enrollment between 2015-16 and 2025-26, while District-run enrollment fell. The mix includes small neighborhood charters, large networks and a number of schools converted from District management. [1]

3 Funding and the fiscal debate

Charter schools do not charge tuition. The District pays each charter a per-pupil amount set by a state formula based on the District's own spending, with separate rates for special education. The District and its supporters argue that stranded fixed costs remain when students leave for charters, contributing to budget pressure; charter operators and parents counter that the schools educate public-school children at lower per-pupil cost and that families choose them. Both arguments appeared during the District's 2026 deficit debate over a budget of about $4.6 billion. Reform proposals at the state level, including changes to the funding formula and to cyber-charter tuition, have been debated in Harrisburg for years. [3]

4 Oversight, renewal and closure

The District's Charter Schools Office evaluates schools on academic performance, financial health and organizational compliance and makes recommendations to the Board. The Board has declined to renew or has moved to revoke charters at schools with weak academic or financial results, and those schools have appealed. Staff, families and neighbors typically testify at public hearings, and decisions can end in settlements or negotiated charter amendments, including enrollment caps. [2]

5 Who holds influence

The Board and Charter Schools Office hold authorizing power; the state legislature controls the law and funding formulas; and the Philadelphia Federation of Teachers has long sought stronger limits on charter growth, though some charter staff are also union-represented. Charter networks and trade associations lobby for stable funding. Philanthropies have funded both District and charter initiatives (see Philanthropy in Philadelphia), and the mayor's appointments to the Board shape authorizing priorities.

6 Information for families

Charter admission is by application and lottery rather than by address. Families can find school profiles and performance data on the District's website and the state's Future Ready PA Index. Complaints about a charter go first to the school's board, then to the District's Charter Schools Office, and, for cyber charters, to the state Department of Education. See How to File a Complaint.
