Faith Communities and Black Clergy

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
Faith Communities and Black Clergy
TypeReligious congregations and clergy associations (private, tax-exempt)
Key organizationBlack Clergy of Philadelphia and Vicinity (Bishop J. Louis Felton reported as president in January 2024; Rev. Robert Collier was president from 2020)
Political roleCandidate forums, endorsements by clergy bodies, public-safety summits, voter engagement
Legal limitChurches as 501(c)(3) organizations may not endorse candidates as organizations

Faith communities are among the oldest and most extensive civic networks in Philadelphia, from the Quaker founders to Black churches that have served as centers of community life since the 18th century. The Free African Society and Mother Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church were founded in Philadelphia in the 1790s. Today congregations of many faiths provide social services, host community meetings and polling places, and take part in political and policy debates. The best-known political formation is the network of Black clergy. [1]

1 Historic role

Black churches in Philadelphia were centers of mutual aid, education, abolitionist organizing and, later, civil-rights and labor organizing. Their pastors often served as community spokespeople when formal political access was limited. This history is the background to the continuing practice of candidates seeking pulpit and clergy-breakfast appearances. See also Philadelphia Political History.

2 Black Clergy of Philadelphia and Vicinity

The Black Clergy of Philadelphia and Vicinity is a long-standing association of pastors from Black congregations. Pennsylvania Capital-Star has described its members as influential in the city's mayoral politics, and local press has reported leadership changes: the Rev. Robert Collier served as president from 2020, and the Philadelphia Tribune reported in January 2024 that Bishop J. Louis Felton was the group's new president. Later leadership changes, if any, should be checked with the organization. The group holds breakfast meetings with candidates, issues statements and hosts summits, including a public-safety summit at Mt. Zion Baptist Church in North Philadelphia that Cherelle Parker, then the Democratic mayoral nominee, was invited to attend. [2]

3 Elections and politics

In the 2023 mayoral race the Black Clergy of Philadelphia endorsed Cherelle Parker, as the Tribune reported. As described in Political Parties in Philadelphia and Ward Leaders and Committeepersons, ward organizations, unions and clergy overlap in Black-majority wards, where church congregations serve as networks for voter mobilization and turnout. Candidates for mayor, Council and judge routinely appear at church forums. Influence varies: an endorsement from a clergy body is one signal among many, and its effect on turnout is debated. [3]

4 Social services and public safety

Congregations run food pantries, youth programs, senior services and housing ministries, and some partner with the City on programs such as the Office of Violence Prevention, the Office of Homeless Services and health outreach by the Department of Public Health. Clergy have taken public positions on gun violence, policing and school policy, and some have joined police-district community advisory councils. Clergy have not been of one mind: on issues such as police oversight, school closures and housing policy, individual pastors have taken different sides.

5 Other faith networks

Philadelphia also has large Catholic, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu and evangelical communities, multi-faith coalitions that advocate on poverty and housing, and faith-based organizing networks such as POWER Interfaith, which in March 2026 was reported by the Inquirer to be calling on City Hall to address the city's affordability crisis. Archdiocesan and Jewish federation institutions run hospitals, schools and social agencies, and many congregations are also landlords of significant property, which links them to zoning and land-use debates. Religious institutions are generally exempt from property tax.

6 Legal boundaries and debate

Under federal tax law, congregations that hold 501(c)(3) status may not endorse or oppose candidates as organizations, though individual clergy may speak personally. Supporters of clergy engagement argue that faith leaders speak for residents who are otherwise underrepresented; critics argue that elected officials can rely too heavily on a small number of leaders as proxies for broad communities. See also Progressive and Grassroots Politics.
