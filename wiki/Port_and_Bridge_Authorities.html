Port and Bridge Authorities

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works
Port and Bridge Authorities
TypeState and bi-state public authorities (not city departments)
PhilaPortPennsylvania port authority (Philadelphia Regional Port Authority); Rich Lazer, Executive Director and CEO since June 1, 2026
DRPADelaware River Port Authority, bi-state (Pennsylvania and New Jersey); John T. Hanson, CEO (as of 2026)

Websitesphilaport.com; drpa.org; drjtbc.org

The port and bridge authorities of the Philadelphia area are public bodies that own and operate the region's marine terminals and many of its Delaware River crossings. None is a city department. PhilaPort is a Pennsylvania-created authority, while the Delaware River Port Authority and the Delaware River Joint Toll Bridge Commission are bi-state agencies created by interstate compacts with Congressional consent. They matter to Philadelphia because they shape freight, jobs, commuting and regional economic development, and because the city has a voice, but not control, in their governance.

1 PhilaPort

PhilaPort, formally the Philadelphia Regional Port Authority, owns marine terminals and warehouses along the Delaware River and oversees the region's seaport.[1] Its board in 2026 included Chairman Michael K. Pearson and other members, among them Philadelphia's representative.[2] After a decade as CEO, Jeff Theobald announced his retirement; the board selected Rich Lazer, 41, then executive director of the Philadelphia Parking Authority and a former deputy mayor for labor, as executive director and CEO effective June 1, 2026. Lazer had served on the PhilaPort board since 2019 as the city's representative. Theobald's reported salary was $420,716.[1] The Inquirer reported that PhilaPort handled a record 889,000 container units in 2025, purchased 150 acres from Norfolk Southern Corporation for $90 million, and has a cruise terminal under construction for Norwegian Cruise Line, while facing competition from ports in Baltimore, New York and Delaware. Port development is tied to labor and economic development (see Organized Labor in Philadelphia and Philadelphia Industrial Development Corporation).

2 Delaware River Port Authority

The Delaware River Port Authority (DRPA) is a bi-state agency of Pennsylvania and New Jersey, which succeeded the Delaware River Joint Commission, created in 1919 to build the first Delaware River bridge. The two states approved the compact creating the authority in 1951, and Congress's consent was signed in July 1952.[5] It operates four toll bridges between Philadelphia-area Pennsylvania counties and southern New Jersey: the Benjamin Franklin, Walt Whitman, Commodore Barry and Betsy Ross bridges, and runs the PATCO Speedline rail service between Philadelphia and Camden County, New Jersey. John T. Hanson is listed as CEO in 2026 board materials.[3] The Benjamin Franklin Bridge opened in 1926, the Walt Whitman in 1957, the Commodore Barry in 1974 and the Betsy Ross in 1976. DRPA tolls are collected in one direction, westbound into Pennsylvania. Revenue pays for bridge and PATCO operations and capital needs, and the agency has also funded economic development and port-related projects, which critics have at times questioned as an appropriate use of toll revenue.

3 Delaware River Joint Toll Bridge Commission

The Delaware River Joint Toll Bridge Commission (DRJTBC) is a separate bi-state agency that operates numerous bridges along the Delaware River north of Philadelphia, including crossings in Bucks County. Its Pennsylvania appointees as of December 2025 meeting minutes were Pamela Janvey (Vice Chair), Daniella Young, Ismail A. Shahid, Daniel Grace (Secretary) and John D. Christy. New Jersey members are nominated by the governor and confirmed by the state Senate, while Pennsylvania members are appointed by the governor without Senate confirmation under the cited summary.[4] Other crossings are run by additional bodies such as county or state bridge commissions.

4 Governance and accountability

Because the authorities are created by state law or interstate compact, changes to their structure usually require action by the legislatures of both states and sometimes Congress (see Pennsylvania State Government and Philadelphia and Federal Government and Philadelphia). City officials sit on or help select some boards, but the governors and legislatures have greater control. Oversight comes from audits by state officials, bond rating agencies and the press. Open-meetings and records rules differ by authority and state; PhilaPort and Pennsylvania appointees are subject to Pennsylvania law (see Sunshine Act and Open Meetings and Right-to-Know Law and Public Records).

5 Public input and complaints

Each body publishes board agendas and minutes, and the public can generally attend meetings or submit comments. Toll and transit complaints go to the authority directly. The SEPTA and PATCO systems are separate from each other, so transit riders should contact the correct operator. Questions about port jobs and development may be raised through City Council members, the Commerce Department or state legislators (see How to Find Your Elected Officials). The Philadelphia airport is covered in its own article.
