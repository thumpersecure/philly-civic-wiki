Political Corruption Cases in Philadelphia

From the Philadelphia Civic Wiki, the free guide to how the city works

This article summarizes several widely reported political corruption cases in Philadelphia, limited to what is on the public record: charges, verdicts, sentences and appeals. It is not a complete list. Federal prosecutors from the U.S. Attorney's Office have brought most of these cases; the city's own watchdogs are described in Board of Ethics and Office of the Inspector General.[1]

1 Vincent Fumo

Vincent Fumo, a longtime Democratic state senator from South Philadelphia, was convicted in federal court on March 16, 2009, on 137 counts including conspiracy, fraud and obstruction of justice, relating to the use of state resources and funds from a nonprofit for personal and political purposes. On July 14, 2009, he was sentenced to 55 months in federal prison; in November 2011 the sentence was lengthened by six months after judicial review. He was released in 2013, according to published accounts.[2]

2 Chaka Fattah

U.S. Rep. Chaka Fattah was convicted on June 21, 2016, of racketeering conspiracy, bribery, bank fraud and other counts, and resigned from Congress. On December 12, 2016, he was sentenced to 10 years in prison. In 2018 an appeals court vacated some of the bribery convictions in light of the U.S. Supreme Court's decision in McDonnell v. United States, and in July 2019 he was resentenced to 10 years. In 2020 he was released from federal prison; the Inquirer reported in July 2020 that federal officials would not say why he came home early.[3]

3 John Dougherty and Bobby Henon

John Dougherty, business manager of IBEW Local 98, and City Councilmember Bobby Henon were charged in a federal case alleging that Henon took official actions in exchange for a union salary; both denied the allegations at trial. On November 15, 2021, a jury convicted Henon on 10 of 18 counts and convicted Dougherty on conspiracy and wire-fraud counts. Henon was reportedly sentenced to about 3.5 years in prison and reported to federal prison in April 2023. In December 2023, Dougherty was separately convicted on embezzlement counts together with other union officials. In July 2024 he was sentenced to six years in prison (72 months), and he surrendered on October 1, 2024. On September 3, 2026, news outlets including CBS News and NBC News reported, citing Dougherty's lawyer, that President Trump had commuted his sentence; he was reported released from prison on September 4, 2026.[4][5] The case prompted debate about the relationship between unions and elected officials; see Labor and Politics in Philadelphia.

4 Kenyatta Johnson

In 2019 federal prosecutors charged then-Councilmember Kenyatta Johnson and his wife, Dawn Chavous, along with two executives of the nonprofit Universal Companies, alleging a scheme in which payments were exchanged for official help with real estate; the defendants denied the charges. A first trial ended in a mistrial on April 20, 2022, when the jury deadlocked. At a retrial, a jury acquitted Johnson and Chavous on November 2, 2022.[6] Johnson was elected Council President in January 2024; see Philadelphia City Council.

5 Ozzie Myers and election fraud

Former U.S. Rep. Michael "Ozzie" Myers, who had been convicted in the 1980 Abscam bribery case, pleaded guilty in 2022 to conspiracy to deprive voters of civil rights and related charges, admitting that he paid election officials to add votes for candidates in South Philadelphia ward divisions in several elections between 2014 and 2018. He was sentenced on September 27, 2022, to 30 months in prison, according to news reports and the U.S. Department of Justice.[7] A former Judge of Elections was also prosecuted. See Elections in Philadelphia.

6 Reform responses

Corruption investigations, including inquiries during the early 2000s into city contracting, contributed to the strengthening of city ethics rules, the creation of the Board of Ethics, contribution limits and expanded powers for the Inspector General. See Conflicts of Interest and Pay-to-Play, Campaign Finance in Philadelphia and Good Government Groups. To report suspected misconduct, see How to Report Corruption or Misconduct.
